That's more than three times the protein for the dollar, from a jar that costs less than the almond bag.

## Sodium, on one ruler

[21 CFR 101.61](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-D/section-101.61) is where FDA defines the sodium claims. Low sodium is 140 mg or less per reference amount customarily consumed. Very low sodium is 35 mg or less per reference amount, and a food with a reference amount of 30 grams or less also has to clear 35 mg per 50 grams. Sodium free is less than 5 mg per reference amount and per labeled serving, and the food can't include a salt ingredient unless the label admits the sodium is trivial. Meal and main-dish products use 140 mg per 100 grams for the low-sodium line.

This table uses milligrams per 100 grams so a spoon of peanut butter and a handful of almonds share a scale. It isn't the labeled serving, and it isn't a claim I'm putting on a jar. Almonds at 1 mg per 100 grams clear the numeric sodium-free line on that scale. Sunflower kernels at 9 mg, chia at 16 mg, and flaxseed at 30 mg clear the 35 mg line on that same scale and miss the under-5 line. Salted peanut butter at 426 mg and salted peanuts at 410 mg miss 140 mg by enough that the jar isn't a low-sodium project.

The regulation also says salt and sodium aren't the same word. Salt means sodium chloride. An unsalted jar can still carry sodium, and if it isn't sodium free the label has to say it isn't. We priced the salted peanut butter. The 17 mg without-salt record is real, and it isn't the $4.97 jar.

The wider list that keeps foods at or under 140 mg per 100 grams, beans included, is the [low-sodium high-protein ranking](/low-sodium-high-protein-foods-per-dollar/). Almonds show up there too, near the expensive end.

## Chia and flax aren't a protein row I stored

Chia seeds are the fiber bargain and a protein blank. [FDC 170554](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170554/nutrients) lists 16.54 grams of protein per 100 grams. The fiber file's bag is 32 ounces, 907 grams, at $9.44, edible fraction 1. Multiply 16.54 through that bag and you get 15.9 grams of protein per dollar, rounded half up to one decimal. The [dollar comparison](/one-dollar-of-protein-vs-one-dollar-of-fiber/) already did that arithmetic and labeled it as arithmetic. I won't paste 15.9 into the chart and pretend the protein file had a row.

Whole flaxseed is 28.5 grams of fiber per dollar and 30 mg of sodium per 100 grams ([FDC 169414](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/169414/nutrients)). The protein sheets I used don't publish grams of flax protein per 100 grams. No cell, no bar. A seed can be useful and still be missing from a file.

Both bags are a different kind of expensive. Chia is $9.44 for 32 ounces. Flax is $5.98 for 22 ounces. You buy them for the fiber, or you don't. Peanut butter at $4.97 for 40 ounces is doing a different job, and the salt came along for the ride.

## What I'd actually buy

Peanut butter, when the goal is protein and the jar can live in a cupboard. The same jar is on the [shelf-stable pantry ranking](/cheapest-shelf-stable-pantry-foods-for-protein/), where flour and dry lentils still beat it and almonds are last in a longer list. Dry roasted peanuts, when the protein has to fit in a coat pocket. Chia, when I want fiber and I've already decided the $9.44 bag is the plan.

Almonds at $6.47 for a pound are how a snack drawer gets expensive while looking careful. 14.8 grams of protein per dollar and 8.8 grams of fiber ([FDC 170567](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170567/nutrients)). The sodium is the one thing they win, at 1 mg per 100 grams. Buy them because you want almonds. Don't ask them to be the cheap plan.
