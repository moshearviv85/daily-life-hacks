The bone looks like a ripoff until you price what you can actually eat. I used to put the drumstick bag back because a third of it was going in the trash. Then I ran the edible grams. The bag was still the cheap protein, bone and all, because the bone was never in the protein number.

Bone-in chicken drumsticks return 50.3 grams of protein per dollar after the bone is removed. Boneless skinless breast is 24.6. Ground beef, 80/20, is 11.3. Same file, same July 2026 prices. The drumstick is about twice the breast and about 4.4 times the beef, and that's the edible figure, not the fairy tale where you chew the knuckle. The wider meat case, eggs included, is the [animal protein per dollar ranking](/animal-protein-per-dollar-ranked/).

## Bone-in versus boneless, after the bone is already gone

The numbers are meat-per-dollar-protein-ranked-2026.csv. The edible fraction next to them is protein-per-dollar-2026.csv. Drumsticks are [FDC 172373](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/172373/nutrients), raw meat and skin, with USDA SR28 refuse of 33 percent bone and cartilage. Edible share of the bag is 67 percent. The 50.3 grams per dollar already uses that 0.67. Boneless thighs are [FDC 173627](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173627/nutrients). Breast is [FDC 171077](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/171077/nutrients). Pork shoulder is [FDC 167849](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/167849/nutrients). Ground beef, 80/20, is [FDC 174036](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/174036/nutrients). Those four are edible fraction 1.00. The package is the food.

50.3 divided by 24.6 is 2.04. Call it about twice. 50.3 divided by 11.3 is 4.45, which is the "about 4.4 times" people round toward when they want one decimal and a little humility. Thighs at 27.7 still beat breast. They don't beat the drumstick. Pork shoulder at 25.2 sits with the breast, not with the bag of legs.

Shoppers keep re-asking it in the meat case anyway, usually while holding both trays.

## Cooking shrinks the piece, not the receipt

Raw edible drumstick meat and skin is what the 18.08 grams of protein per 100 grams on that FDC record describes. You don't serve it raw. Roasting and braising drive water off, and the [USDA cooking-yield table for meat and poultry](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400525/Data/retn/USDA_CookingYields_MeatPoultry02.pdf) is how they measure the weight that's left. Yield there is cooked weight over raw weight. It's a smaller piece. It's not a coupon that expires.

Protein doesn't evaporate with the steam in any way that would hand the dollar ranking to the boneless tray. The refuse definition itself is in the [SR28 documentation](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400525/Data/SR/SR28/sr28_doc.pdf): refuse is the inedible share of the item as purchased, and the edible portion is what's left. Bone and cartilage are that share on the drumstick. Skin stays in this record because the row is meat and skin, not meat only. If you peel the skin off, you have a different food and a different gram weight. Don't pretend the 50.3 was a skinless promise.

I won't invent a second percent and knock it off the 50.3. The file already did the bone. Cooking changes how the plate looks. The [plant versus animal protein page](/plant-vs-animal-protein-cost-per-dollar/) is the next argument, the one about lentils and a quality score, and it still has to live with this drumstick number.

## Ground beef is not the cheap meat protein

Hamburger has the reputation. The file has 11.3 grams per dollar for 80/20, at $6.89 a pound, BLS U.S. average, July 2026 ([FDC 174036](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/174036/nutrients)). Drumsticks are a $5.46 bag for five pounds. The beef is edible from the first gram of the package. It's also the short bar.

Thirty grams of protein, the portion I use so the cuts share a plate, is 30 divided by the grams-per-dollar figure, rounded half up to the cent. Drumsticks are $0.60. Thighs are $1.08. Pork shoulder is $1.19. Breast is $1.22. Ground beef is $2.65 ([FDC 174036](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/174036/nutrients)). Same 30 grams. The beef portion costs $2.05 more than the drumsticks. That's the whole personality of the tray.

Beans and tofu sit in a different cooler, and the per-serving comparison with chicken is [protein per serving for beans, chicken, and tofu](/protein-per-serving-beans-chicken-tofu-compared/). If the question is only which meat package wins, the beef isn't it.

## What I buy when both trays are in the case

Thighs when I want them done on a Tuesday without a bone on the cutting board. Breast when the recipe is picky and the $1.22 for 30 grams ([FDC 171077](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/171077/nutrients)) doesn't ruin the plan. Drumsticks when the goal is protein per dollar and I have a sheet pan and 20 minutes of not minding a bone. The bag's 67 percent edible share is the reason the sticker looks worse than the edible math. You're paying $1.09 a purchased pound and eating the 67 percent that has the protein.

None of that buys extra days in the fridge. Cooked poultry is still a short clock, and the day count lives on [how long cooked meat and poultry last in the fridge](/how-long-do-cooked-meat-and-poultry-last-in-the-fridge/). A cheap drumstick that sits until Friday was not a bargain. Freeze what you won't eat inside the window, or buy the smaller bag.

The boneless tray is convenience with a price. The bone-in bag is the better protein dollar after the bone is already out of the math. I still want the burger sometimes. I just stopped calling it the cheap one.
