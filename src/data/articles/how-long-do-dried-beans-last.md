Kept dry, sealed, and out of the heat, dried beans stay wholesome well past the date printed on the bag. The $3.97 bag of pinto beans in your cupboard is holding roughly 389 grams of protein and 281 grams of fiber, and it is in no hurry.

What changes with age is not safety. It's cooking time, and sometimes patience.

## How long do dried beans last uncooked?

Longer than the bag implies. The FDA is direct about what those dates mean: [a use by date is the manufacturer's recommendation for best flavor or quality, and it is not a food safety date](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/are-you-storing-food-safely). At some point past it a product may shift in taste, color, texture, or nutrient content while still being fine to eat.

Dried beans are about as forgiving as food gets on this front, because almost all the water is already gone and bacteria need water to do anything. That's the same property that makes them the backbone of our [shelf stable pantry ranking](/shelf-stable-pantry-per-dollar/).

So I don't throw out beans because of a date. I throw them out because of what I find when I open the bag.

## Do dried beans go bad?

They do, just not on a schedule. What ruins dried beans is moisture, heat, and bugs.

The signs are obvious once you look. Clumping, a damp or musty smell, visible mold, tiny holes in the beans, or anything moving. The FDA's position on mold is that it's a sign of spoilage and the safest practice is to discard food that's moldy, and I'd apply that to a bag of beans without a second thought. Beans are too cheap to gamble on.

Beans that are simply old, dull looking, and a bit dusty are a different story. Those are a quality problem, and a quality problem in a bean means a slow dinner.

## Why do old beans refuse to soften?

Because they keep drying out, and past a certain point they stop taking water back.

I've had a bag of chickpeas that lived through two apartment moves go into a pot, simmer for ninety minutes, and come out with a chalky center that never gave in. That's not spoilage. That's a bean that dried past the point of return, and no amount of stubbornness on your side fixes it.

Two things help. A long soak helps most, and [the same day quick soak method](/how-to-quick-soak-dried-beans-same-day/) helps when you forgot. Pressure helps more than either. If you're working through the basics of getting a pot of beans right, [how to cook dried beans from scratch](/how-to-cook-dried-beans-from-scratch/) covers the sequence.

Salt in the cooking water is fine. Acid is not, at least not early. Tomatoes and vinegar go in after the beans are tender, or the skins stay tight for hours.

## How should you store dried beans?

Cool, dark, dry, and sealed. That's it, and the bag they came in is the weak link.

I decant into glass jars, partly for the seal and partly because I can see what I have. A clear jar of split peas on a shelf gets used. A half open plastic bag folded over behind the flour does not. The FDA also suggests keeping non-perishable foods away from household cleaning products and chemicals, which sounds obvious right up until you look at what's actually under your sink.

Heat is the quiet enemy. The cupboard above the stove is the worst place in the kitchen for beans and the most convenient one, which is exactly how it ends up full of them.

## Is a big bag worth it?

If you have a cool dry corner, yes. The 4 pound bag of pinto beans at $3.97 returned 97.9 grams of protein per dollar, the best figure in our entire 49 food ranking, and it stayed the best across [the plant protein rankings](/plant-protein-per-dollar-ranked/) too. Buying that in a 1 pound bag costs you more per gram for no reason other than the packaging.

The catch is honest. A 4 pound bag is a commitment. If you cook beans twice a month it will outlive the bag it came in, which is fine, but decant it and be realistic about the pace. Storage plans that assume a version of you who cooks beans on Sundays tend to underperform.

Lentil bags are the easiest place to start, and the price difference between them is worth knowing before you fill a shelf, which we went through in [which lentils are the best](/which-lentils-are-the-best/). Green split peas are the sleeper here. At $1.42 a bag they carried about 101 grams of fiber, which is 71.0 grams of fiber per dollar against 52.0 for the $2.67 bag of navy beans, and they turn into [split pea soup](/split-pea-soup-recipe-high-fiber/) without any soaking at all. That's the bag I'd stock first.

Beans keep. That's the entire feature.
