Soak wilted lettuce in ice water for **5 to 10 minutes**, then dry it thoroughly. That's the whole rescue operation. No lemon juice, no special powder, and no solemn goodbye beside the trash can.

This works when the lettuce is limp because it lost water. It doesn't work when the lettuce is slimy, smelly, or turning black. At that point the ice isn't performing a comeback. It's making cold garbage.

| What the lettuce looks like | What to do | Time |
|---|---|---:|
| Limp but clean and fresh-smelling | Soak in ice water, then dry | 5 to 10 minutes |
| Sturdy romaine that's still floppy | Trim the base, soak, then dry | Up to 15 minutes |
| Slimy, mushy, sour-smelling, or blackened | Discard it | 0 minutes |

*Sources: [UC San Diego Center for Community Health](https://communityhealth.ucsd.edu/work/eat-ca/salad-greens) (5-to-10-minute ice-water soak) and [Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/gardening/preparing-lettuce) (ice water revives limp leaves).*

## How do you revive wilted lettuce step by step?

You need a large bowl, ice water, and something that actually dries the leaves afterward. A salad spinner does more useful work here than any $40 countertop gadget with a motivational name.

1. **Sort the leaves.** Pull out anything slimy, mushy, badly discolored, or sour-smelling. Don't negotiate with rot.
2. **Separate and trim.** Pull apart lettuce leaves so the cold water can reach them. For a whole head of romaine, trim a thin slice from the dry base.
3. **Fill a bowl with cold water and ice.** Genuinely cold, not three lonely cubes floating in room-temperature optimism.
4. **Soak for 5 to 10 minutes.** Check delicate greens at 5. Give sturdy romaine up to 15 if it still bends like wet paper.
5. **Drain and dry completely.** Salad spinner or clean towels. Dressing sticks to dry leaves. Wet leaves turn vinaigrette into expensive puddle water.
6. **Use the lettuce the same day.** The ice bath buys another meal. It doesn't restart the lettuce's warranty.

No ice? Very cold tap water still helps, just slower. If leaves are still floppy at 10 minutes, keep going. [America's Test Kitchen](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/articles/6001-how-to-revive-wilted-vegetables) tears the leaves first and soaks a full 30 minutes, well past the 5-to-10-minute window the extension guidance uses. Both are fine. Start short, check the leaf, stop when it snaps instead of drooping. Past half an hour you're mostly making cold, sodden lettuce.

One thing an ice bath is not: a wash. [FDA guidance](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/selecting-and-serving-produce-safely) is to wash produce under running water, and a still bowl isn't that. If the bag says pre-washed or ready-to-eat, the FDA says you can skip washing altogether, so use a clean bowl and clean hands rather than undoing the packer's work with a crusty colander.

Once it's crisp, put it somewhere it matters. A [pear salad with walnuts](/pear-salad-with-walnuts-fiber-rich/) is a better ending than letting the same bag wilt again on Friday.

## Why does ice water make limp lettuce crisp again?

Lettuce is mostly water. [Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/gardening/preparing-lettuce) puts lettuce at 94.9% water, basically a water balloon with opinions. [UC Davis notes](https://postharvest.ucdavis.edu/produce-facts-sheets/lettuce-crisphead) that crisphead lettuce is prone to water loss after harvest, especially in warm, low-humidity conditions, and that the outer leaves soften and lose crispness as that water goes. A short soak lets limp leaves take it back up, and the cold keeps the result salad-appropriate instead of warm and soggy.

That's also why freezing isn't the clever shortcut it sounds like. Illinois Extension says lettuce can't be preserved at home by freezing, canning, or drying, precisely because of that water content. Thawed lettuce is excellent if your goal is to make lettuce feel personally betrayed, but it's not coming back as a crisp salad.

## Which greens can you rescue with an ice bath?

The method works best on greens that are limp but otherwise sound:

- Romaine and iceberg usually show the most obvious improvement.
- Green leaf and red leaf lettuce can perk up quickly.
- Kale and chard may need closer to 10 or 15 minutes.
- Spinach and arugula are delicate, so check them at 5 minutes.
- Parsley and cilantro can recover well after you trim the stem ends.

Spring mix is the unreliable roommate here. Some leaves recover while others already crossed into mush. Sort it before soaking, then dry it gently.

## When should you throw wilted lettuce away?

Wilting and spoilage aren't the same thing. Wilting is a quality problem: the leaf lost water and went floppy. Spoilage is a safety problem, and ice doesn't touch it. A limp leaf that's clean, intact, and fresh-smelling may be worth rescuing. A slimy leaf isn't thirsty. It's finished.

That slime has a name. UC Davis describes bacterial soft rot in lettuce, usually *Erwinia* species, as wet, rotten areas with a strong, unpleasant odor that spread as it progresses. The sour-garbage smell out of the bag isn't your imagination, and it isn't a texture problem.

Throw the lettuce away if you find:

- A sour, rotten, or fermented smell
- Slime across the leaves or inside the bag
- Mushy tissue that tears when you touch it
- Widespread black or brown decay
- Visible mold

The FDA's rule is short enough to memorize: cut away damaged or bruised areas, throw away any produce that looks rotten. Don't salvage the best-looking half of a bag if liquid from rotten leaves has coated everything else. Saving $2.49 isn't a personality test.

## How do you keep lettuce from wilting again?

The second rescue is prevention. Dry the leaves well, store them in a container or bag with a paper towel to catch extra moisture, and keep them in the refrigerator's high-humidity drawer if yours has one. That drawer isn't a gimmick: UC Davis lists optimum relative humidity for crisphead lettuce at above 95%, and your open fridge shelf is nowhere near that. Too little moisture makes lettuce limp. Too much loose water encourages the exact slimy situation we're trying to avoid.

Two numbers worth taping inside the fridge door. The FDA says keep perishable produce like lettuce at 40°F or below, checked with an actual thermometer rather than vibes. UC San Diego keeps salad greens up to 7 days in an airtight container with a damp cloth. Soaked leaves don't get those 7 days. They're wet and handled, so use them that day.

Packing lunch? Keep dressing away from the leaves until you eat. The full system is in this guide to [packing salad for work without making it soggy](/how-to-pack-salad-for-work-not-soggy/), and it costs you one extra container instead of $14 for a desk salad that tastes like its own plastic lid.
