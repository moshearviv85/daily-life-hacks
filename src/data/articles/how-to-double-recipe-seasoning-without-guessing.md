For most savory dishes, double the bulk ingredients but start herbs, spices, and salt at **1.5 times the original amount**. Cook until it's safe to taste, then add more in small steps.

That 1.5x figure isn't kitchen folklore. [Mississippi State University Extension recommends increasing herbs and spices by 1.5x when doubling](https://happyhealthy.extension.msstate.edu/node/45), and [Heart Foundation NZ gives the same starting point for spices and salt](https://www.heartfoundation.org.nz/documents/food-industry/hospitality-hub/recipe-adaptation-guidelines.pdf). Heart Foundation NZ adds the step people skip: taste or assess afterward. A starting point, not a law for every recipe.

## The doubling cheat sheet

| Part of the recipe | Double-batch start | What to do next |
|---|---:|---|
| Meat, vegetables, grains, beans | 2x | Use a roomier pan; cook to doneness cues |
| Broth or water in soups, stews, and sauces | Up to 2x as an initial ceiling | Reserve some; add it as needed to match the original consistency |
| Dried or fresh herbs and ground spices | 1.5x | Taste at the end, raise in small steps |
| Salt | 1.5x | Count sodium from broth, canned food, cheese, sauces |
| Cayenne, red pepper flakes, hot sauce | 1.5x or less | You can add heat, you can't subtract it |
| Vinegar, lemon juice, finishing acids | 1.5x | Add the rest after the dish reduces |
| Baking powder, baking soda, yeast | Recipe-dependent | Two batches are safer; see below |
| Home-canning or pickling proportions | Use a tested recipe exactly | Don't change the tested vinegar, water, and food ratios |

Where those numbers come from: the 1.5x rows for spices and salt are published extension and foodservice guidance. The acid row is our own call, on the same you-can-add-but-can't-subtract logic. Heart Foundation NZ also caps the exercise at a factor of four.

## How do you calculate the new seasoning amounts?

**Original amount × 1.5 = double-batch starting amount**

| Single batch calls for | Start the double batch with |
|---|---|
| 1/4 teaspoon | 3/8 teaspoon (a scant 1/2) |
| 1/2 teaspoon | 3/4 teaspoon |
| 1 teaspoon | 1 1/2 teaspoons |
| 2 teaspoons | 1 tablespoon |
| 1 tablespoon | 1 1/2 tablespoons |

So 1 teaspoon salt, 2 teaspoons cumin, and 1/4 teaspoon cayenne becomes 1 1/2 teaspoons, 1 tablespoon, and 3/8 teaspoon.

Two caveats. Measuring errors get amplified when you scale, which is why Heart Foundation NZ tells cooks to weigh and record spices, salt, and baking powder rather than eyeball them. And the popular claim that chili heat builds "exponentially" gets repeated everywhere with no study attached, so read the cautious cayenne number as risk management, not physics.

Cook food fully before tasting anything with raw meat, poultry, seafood, or eggs in it. The [USDA safe-temperature chart](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/safe-temperature-chart) beats guessing by color.

## What doubles cleanly in a savory recipe?

Solids begin at 2x. One pound of chicken becomes two. Liquid needs a checkpoint instead: if the original uses two cups of broth, four cups is the double-batch ceiling, not an instruction to pour every drop into the pot. Reserve some, add it as the food cooks, and stop when the consistency matches the original. [Heart Foundation NZ notes that a larger pot's lower surface-area-to-volume ratio can slow reduction and may require less added liquid or a more concentrated stock](https://www.heartfoundation.org.nz/documents/food-industry/hospitality-hub/recipe-adaptation-guidelines.pdf). No single holdback percentage fits every pot.

The equipment ceiling is a safety question, not a flavor one. Colorado State University Extension says to [fill a slow cooker no less than half full and no more than two-thirds full](https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/crockpot-and-slow-cooker-food-safety/) for safe and even heat distribution. For electric pressure cookers, CSU Extension says to [stay below the two-thirds "MAX FILL" line, and half full for expanding ingredients like beans or pasta](https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/how-to-convert-slow-cooker-recipes-for-electric-pressure-cooking/). A double batch that doesn't fit is two batches.

Same on the stove: crowd a skillet and you steam food meant to brown. If cost is why you're batching, [beans can stretch ground beef](/ground-beef-vs-beans-protein-cost/). Leave headroom. Dinner shouldn't become a lava experiment.

## How much salt when you double a recipe?

Start at 1.5x the listed salt: 1 teaspoon becomes 1 1/2 teaspoons, not 2. Then read the rest of the ingredient list. Broth, canned tomatoes, cheese, soy sauce, and spice blends bring their own sodium. If several are in the pot, hold back more and taste near the end. [Fixing an oversalted soup](/fix-oversalted-soup-sauce-rice/) takes far more work than adding another pinch.

Salt doesn't get stronger in a big pot. A slower-reducing pot just lands on a different concentration, so let it hit the intended consistency before the final call.

## Does cooking time double when you double a recipe?

No. A larger pot takes longer to boil, reduce, and thicken, and two shallow pans won't behave like one deep pan. Follow the recipe's visual and texture cues, then use a thermometer for meat, poultry, seafood, eggs, and casseroles. The clock is an estimate. Food safety isn't.

Cooling is the part everybody forgets. [USDA guidance says to divide a large pot of soup or stew into small portions in shallow containers](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/refrigeration) before refrigerating it, and Illinois Extension gives the edge: [food left between 40°F and 140°F for over two hours should be thrown out](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/live-well-eat-well/2016-09-16-how-important-2-hour-rule). A stockpot isn't a storage container just because it has handles.

## Baking needs its own scaling rule

Heart Foundation NZ's foodservice guide suggests trying 1.5x baking powder, baking soda, and yeast when doubling, then assessing the result. It also flags that batters and doughs behave quite differently in large mixers compared to home mixers.

Two rules from that guide are the ones home bakers skip. Keep the depth of batter in the pan the same as the original called for, whatever pan you use. And expect larger items to need longer in the oven, sometimes at a lower temperature so the outside doesn't dry out before the middle sets.

None of that is permission to apply 1.5x to every cake or cookie formula. For a home recipe that hasn't been tested at double size, two separate batches are the predictable option. If you do scale one, weigh the small ingredients, note what you changed, and judge doneness rather than doubling the bake time.

## Don't improvise with canning or pickling

Shelf-stable canning is the hard stop. The [National Center for Home Food Preservation says to use tested canning recipes and not alter the proportions of vinegar, food, or water](https://nchfp.uga.edu/how/pickle/general-information-pickling/general-information-on-pickling/). Taste preference doesn't overrule a tested acid ratio. If a trusted recipe offers a larger tested yield, use that. Otherwise run separate batches. The 1.5x shortcut belongs in dinner, not preservation.
