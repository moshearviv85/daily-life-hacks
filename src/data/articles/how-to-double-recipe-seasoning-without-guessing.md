For most savory dishes, double the bulk ingredients but start herbs, spices, and salt at **1.5 times the original amount**. Cook until the food is safe to taste, then add more in small steps if it needs it.

That 1.5x figure isn't kitchen folklore. [Mississippi State University Extension recommends increasing herbs and spices by 1.5x when doubling](https://happyhealthy.extension.msstate.edu/node/45), and [Heart Foundation NZ gives the same starting point for spices and salt](https://www.heartfoundation.org.nz/documents/food-industry/hospitality-hub/recipe-adaptation-guidelines.pdf). Both pieces of guidance leave room to assess the result. Treat 1.5x as a starting point, not a law stamped onto every recipe.

## The doubling cheat sheet

| Part of the recipe | Double-batch starting point | What to do next |
|---|---:|---|
| Meat, vegetables, grains, beans | 2x | Use a pan with enough room and cook to the recipe's doneness cues |
| Broth or water in soups, stews, and sauces | Up to 2x as an initial ceiling | Reserve some; add it as needed to match the original consistency |
| Dried or fresh herbs and ground spices | 1.5x | Finish cooking, taste, and increase in small steps |
| Salt | 1.5x | Account for salty broth, canned food, cheese, and sauces before adding more |
| Cayenne, red pepper flakes, hot sauce | 1.5x or less | Start cautiously because you can add heat but can't subtract it |
| Baking powder, baking soda, yeast | Recipe-dependent | Two separate batches are more predictable; see the baking note below |
| Home-canning or pickling proportions | Use a tested recipe exactly | Don't change the tested vinegar, water, and food ratios |

The solid-ingredient row is ordinary batch math. Liquid depends on the vessel and how fast the larger batch reduces. The 1.5x rows are a deliberate holdback so you can taste and adjust. If the original recipe already says to season "to taste," keep doing that instead of inventing a number.

## A 30-second seasoning calculation

Multiply the original seasoning amount by 1.5:

**Original amount × 1.5 = double-batch starting amount**

If a single batch uses 1 teaspoon salt, 2 teaspoons cumin, and 1/4 teaspoon cayenne, start the double batch with:

* 1 1/2 teaspoons salt
* 3 teaspoons cumin, which is 1 tablespoon
* 3/8 teaspoon cayenne, or a little less if you want room to adjust

Cook the food completely before tasting anything that contains raw meat, poultry, seafood, or eggs. The [USDA safe-temperature chart](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/safe-temperature-chart) beats guessing by color.

## What doubles cleanly in a savory recipe?

Meat, vegetables, grains, and beans usually begin at 2x. If a recipe calls for one pound of chicken, the double batch starts with two pounds.

Broth and water need a checkpoint. If the original dish uses two cups of broth, four cups is the double-batch ceiling, not an instruction to pour every drop into the pot. Reserve some, then add it as the food cooks until the consistency matches the original. [Heart Foundation NZ notes that a larger pot's lower surface-area-to-volume ratio can slow reduction and may require less added liquid or a more concentrated stock](https://www.heartfoundation.org.nz/documents/food-industry/hospitality-hub/recipe-adaptation-guidelines.pdf). There isn't one defensible holdback percentage for every pot, so use the original texture as the target.

The catch is equipment. Double the ingredients in the same cramped skillet and you may steam food that was supposed to brown. Put twice the casserole filling into the same dish and the depth changes, so the original time stops being useful. Use a larger pan, two pans, or two batches to keep the food close to the original depth.

If cost is the reason you're making a bigger batch, [beans can stretch ground beef while keeping protein in the meal](/ground-beef-vs-beans-protein-cost/). Just leave enough headroom in the pot. Dinner shouldn't become a stovetop lava experiment.

## How much salt when you double a recipe?

For a savory double batch, start at 1.5x the listed salt. A recipe with 1 teaspoon starts at 1 1/2 teaspoons, not 2 teaspoons.

Then look at the rest of the ingredient list. Broth, canned tomatoes, cheese, soy sauce, and spice blends can bring their own sodium. If several of those are in the pot, hold back more and taste near the end. If you overshoot, [fixing an oversalted soup or sauce](/fix-oversalted-soup-sauce-rice/) takes far more work than adding another pinch.

This isn't because salt becomes magically stronger in a larger pot. Heart Foundation NZ's scaling guide says a larger pot has a lower surface-area-to-volume ratio, so it can take longer to boil, reduce, and thicken. That changes concentration and timing. Let the dish reach its intended consistency, then make the final seasoning call.

## Does cooking time double when you double a recipe?

No. A larger pot may take longer to boil, reduce, and thicken. Two shallow pans won't behave like one deep pan. Oven load and pan position can change browning.

Follow the recipe's visual and texture cues, then use a thermometer for meat, poultry, seafood, eggs, and casseroles. The clock is an estimate. Food safety isn't.

One more batch-cooking detail matters: cooling. [USDA guidance says to divide a large pot of soup or stew into small portions in shallow containers](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/refrigeration) before refrigerating it. A stockpot isn't a storage container just because it has handles.

## Baking needs its own scaling rule

Heart Foundation NZ's foodservice guide suggests trying 1.5x baking powder, baking soda, and yeast when doubling, then assessing the result. It also warns that batters and doughs behave differently as equipment and batch size change.

That's useful guidance, but it isn't permission to apply 1.5x to every cake, bread, or cookie formula. For a home recipe that hasn't been tested at double size, two separate batches are the predictable option. If you do scale one batch, keep the pan depth close to the original, weigh small ingredients carefully, record what you changed, and judge doneness rather than doubling the bake time.

## Don't improvise with canning or pickling

Shelf-stable canning is the hard stop. The [National Center for Home Food Preservation says to use tested canning recipes and not alter the proportions of vinegar, food, or water](https://nchfp.uga.edu/how/pickle/general-information-pickling/general-information-on-pickling/). Flavor preference doesn't overrule a tested acid ratio.

If a trusted canning recipe provides a larger tested yield, follow that version. Otherwise, run the tested recipe in separate batches. The 1.5x seasoning shortcut belongs in dinner, not in safety-critical preservation.

For the actual dinner, keep it simple: double the bulk, start the strong seasonings at 1.5x, and taste when it's safe. If the pot still tastes flat, [use acid, herbs, browning, or measured umami before adding more salt](/big-flavor-less-salt-citrus-herbs-umami-swaps/). That's a better plan than turning the salt jar into a panic button.
