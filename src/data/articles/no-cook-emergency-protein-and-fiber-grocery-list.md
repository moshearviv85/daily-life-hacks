The lights go out, the stove is a cold slab, and the fridge starts its slow climb toward the kitchen air. That's the night "just cook the lentils" stops being advice. You need food you can open with a hand and a spoon.

Peanut butter is the protein bargain that doesn't care about the burner: 50.7 g per dollar, a 40 oz jar at $4.97 ([FDC 174266](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/174266/nutrients)). Canned black beans are the fiber bargain sitting next to it: 34.4 g of fiber per dollar and 30.1 g of protein, a 15.5 oz can at $0.88 ([FDC 175188](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/175188/nutrients)). Whole milk, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, and a rotisserie bird are no-cook too, and they still need the cold. [USDA FSIS](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/danger-zone-40f-140f) says perishable food shouldn't sit out of refrigeration more than 2 hours, or 1 hour above 90°F.

## Which no-cook groceries deliver the cheapest protein and fiber safely without a stove or fridge?

Eight of these twelve can live in a cupboard. Four can't. The chart's missing bar means that file has no row. It isn't a measured zero, and I won't pretend the milk grew fiber while the power was out.

Read the rank as a cupboard, not a virtue contest. Peanut butter wins protein per dollar and still brings 11.4 g of fiber per dollar. The beans lose that protein race and then lap everyone on fiber. Carrots, prunes, and apples aren't pretending to be dinner. They're the fiber you can chew while the can heats up in your hand, which it won't, because you aren't heating it.

## The pantry cart, when the fridge is just a warm cabinet

If I had to shop for an outage with one basket, I'd take the jar, the peanuts, the sunflower seeds, two cans of black beans, a can of tuna, a bag of carrots, and the apples. Prunes if the bag is already in the cart. None of that needs a burner. None of it needs the fridge while the package is still closed.

The peanut row is salted, and the price file says so. [FDC 174266](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/174266/nutrients) is smooth peanut butter with salt, 22.21 g of protein and 5.0 g of fiber per 100 g. The note on that row says the unsalted twin, FDC 172470, matches the protein and the fiber and drops the sodium from 426 mg to 17 mg per 100 g. Buy the jar you'll actually finish. Don't let me pretend the salted one is a shy little condiment.

Dry roasted peanuts do the same trick one aisle over. [FDC 174262](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/174262/nutrients) is 24.35 g of protein per 100 g and 39.8 g per dollar, from a 16 oz jar at $2.78. The fiber sheet gives them 8.4 g of fiber per 100 g, which is 13.7 g per dollar. Sunflower kernels are the calmer salt story: 9 mg of sodium per 100 g on the sodium sheet, 20.78 g of protein per 100 g, 31.7 g of protein per dollar, and 13.1 g of fiber per dollar ([FDC 170562](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170562/nutrients)). A 16 oz bag was $2.98. That's a snack that doesn't ask the stove for permission.

Canned black beans need a footnote before anyone quotes them at a potluck that isn't happening. [FDC 175188](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/175188/nutrients) is black turtle beans, canned, and the protein sheet flags the row as a proxy because USDA names the cultivar rather than the store label. The can in the price file is the whole 15.5 oz, undrained, at $0.88. Protein is 6.03 g per 100 g. The same note says the low-sodium twin has the same protein and a different sodium number, 384 mg versus 138 mg per 100 g. Fiber is 6.9 g per 100 g, and that's the 34.4 g per dollar. If you'd rather boil the dry bag when the stove comes back, the [dried beans converter](/tools/dried-beans-to-canned-converter/) is the half-cup math. Tonight, the can is the meal.

Tuna is the pantry protein with the least romance. Chunk light in water, drained, is 19.44 g of protein per 100 g and 22.4 g per dollar ([FDC 173709](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173709/nutrients)). The sodium sheet puts that record at 247 mg per 100 g. A 5 oz can was $0.98. Eat it from the can if you must. I won't clap.

Carrots, prunes, and apples are on the fiber sheet and absent from the no-cook protein sheet, so they don't get a protein crown here. Whole bagged carrots are 16.1 g of fiber per dollar, 2.8 g per 100 g, a 2 lb bag at $1.58 ([FDC 170393](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170393/nutrients)). Prunes are 8.1 g of fiber per dollar and 7.1 g per 100 g ([FDC 168162](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168162/nutrients)). Gala apples are 7.3 g per dollar, and the fiber row uses an edible fraction of 0.9 so the core isn't counted as lunch ([FDC 168204](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168204/nutrients)). The wider produce ranking is [cheapest high-fiber fruits and vegetables](/cheapest-high-fiber-fruits-and-vegetables/). This page only keeps the ones you can eat with the stove off.

## The four foods that still need a cold chain

Whole milk, plain nonfat Greek yogurt, 4% cottage cheese, and the rotisserie bird are no-cook in a working kitchen and a bad plan in a dead fridge. I'm not going to rebrand them as pantry food because a spoon fits in the tub.

Milk is 28.5 g of protein per dollar, 3.15 g per 100 g, a gallon at $4.31 in the July 2026 BLS average ([FDC 171265](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/171265/nutrients)). Greek yogurt is 27.5 g per dollar, 10.19 g per 100 g, a 32 oz tub at $3.36 ([FDC 170894](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170894/nutrients)). Cottage cheese is 26.3 g per dollar, 11.12 g per 100 g, a 24 oz tub at $2.87 ([FDC 172179](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/172179/nutrients)). None of those three has a fiber row. Don't invent one.

The bird looks generous until you take the bones off the scale. [FDC 171054](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/171054/nutrients) is roasted chicken, meat only, 28.93 g of protein per 100 g. The price is a whole cooked bird, about 36 oz, at $5.97, and the protein sheet's edible fraction is 0.54 because USDA's refuse note pulls off 33% bone and 13% skin. The 26.7 g per dollar is the meat. It isn't the weight of the carcass you paid for. Useful if the fridge holds. Useless as a cupboard item once it cools into the gap FSIS calls the danger zone, 40°F to 140°F.

FSIS wants cold food at or below 40°F and hot food at or above 140°F. Perishable food out of the fridge gets 2 hours, then it's done. Above 90°F, that window is 1 hour. Leftovers, including an opened can, go back to 40°F within those 2 hours. A sealed can doesn't start that clock. A cracked can, a warm tub of yogurt, and a bird that's been riding in the front seat since the deli do.

## What one dollar of each food actually buys

A dollar of peanut butter is 50.7 g of protein and 11.4 g of fiber. A dollar of dry roasted peanuts is 39.8 g of protein and 13.7 g of fiber. A dollar of sunflower kernels is 31.7 g and 13.1 g. A dollar of canned black beans is 30.1 g of protein and 34.4 g of fiber, which is the rude little victory of the whole list. You came for protein and the can handed you more fiber than the jar.

A dollar of milk is 28.5 g of protein. Yogurt is 27.5 g. The rotisserie meat is 26.7 g. Cottage cheese is 26.3 g. Tuna is 22.4 g. Carrots are 16.1 g of fiber with no protein figure on the no-cook sheet. Prunes are 8.1 g of fiber. Apples are 7.3 g. I wouldn't build an outage dinner out of apples alone unless the plan is to be hungry and technically correct.

The canned bean is also the one that translates when the power returns and you feel like cooking again. Dry beans cost less per gram once you simmer them, and the [dried beans converter](/tools/dried-beans-to-canned-converter/) already has the crowd math. This list is the version for the night the pot stays in the cabinet.

## Where these numbers come from

Twelve foods. Protein for the nine that appear in no-cook-protein-per-dollar-2026.csv. Fiber for the seven that appear in fiber-per-dollar-2026.csv. The snack file, high-fiber-snacks-per-dollar-2026.csv, repeats the fiber-per-dollar figure for peanuts, sunflower kernels, peanut butter, carrots, prunes, and apples. Prices are the audited basket already in those files, Walmart and the July 2026 BLS milk average, not a new walk through the store. Edible portion, proxies, and the BLS series are on the [methodology page](/methodology/).

Storage is the part the spreadsheet can't taste. Pantry, on this page, means the package doesn't need a fridge while it's sealed: the nut jar, the sunflower bag, the prune bag, the whole carrots, the whole apples, and the unopened cans. Cold chain means milk, yogurt, cottage cheese, and the rotisserie bird. FSIS wrote the 2-hour rule for that second group, and for leftovers after you open the first.
