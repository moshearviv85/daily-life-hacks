How long homemade salad dressing lasts depends on the jar. A plain vinaigrette keeps up to 2 weeks refrigerated. Fresh garlic or herbs bring that to about 1 week. Dairy, buttermilk, and mayo-based dressings last 3 to 4 days. Raw egg dressing is a same-day job. Opened store-bought dressing keeps about 1 to 3 months in the fridge.

Raw garlic or fresh herbs packed in oil, with no vinegar or other acid, isn't one of those quality windows. Oregon State University Extension says low-acid food in oil leaves an oxygen-free spot where Clostridium botulinum can grow, so refrigerate that jar and use it within 4 days, or freeze it. A splash of lemon isn't the tested fix.

Store-bought Zesty Italian can sit in a pantry for a year and shrug. Your mason jar of lemon-garlic vinaigrette cannot. Homemade dressing is fresh, once-living ingredients with nothing holding the line behind them, unlike the bottled stuff packed with stabilizers and more sugar than you'd guess.

Most of the time this is a waste-and-taste question. Once in a while, specifically when raw garlic meets a lot of oil, it's an actual food safety question. Here's how to tell those apart.

## How long does homemade salad dressing last?

| Dressing type | Fridge life | Where the number comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Vinaigrette (oil, vinegar, dried herbs and spices) | Up to 2 weeks | Ask Extension, through Cooperative Extension: simple vinaigrettes can be kept refrigerated for up to 2 weeks ([the answer](https://ask.extension.org/kb/faq.php?id=292098)) |
| With fresh garlic or herbs | Up to 1 week | The same Ask Extension answer gives up to 1 week for dressings made with fresh ingredients, like onions and herbs. University of Maine Extension's basic oil-and-acid card also says use within 7 days ([Make Your Own: Salad Dressing](https://extension.umaine.edu/food-health/efnep/make-your-own/salad-dressing/)) |
| Dairy or buttermilk | 3 to 4 days | University of Maine Cooperative Extension's ranch card is mayonnaise plus buttermilk, yogurt, or sour cream, and it says use leftovers within 4 days ([Ranch Salad Dressing](https://extension.umaine.edu/food-health/recipes/ranch-salad-dressing/)). There isn't a separate extension line for buttermilk alone, so this row uses that 4-day card |
| Mayo-based | 3 to 4 days | That same Maine ranch card is half mayonnaise and says 4 days. FoodSafety.gov gives 3 to 4 days for egg, chicken, tuna, and macaroni salads, the closest mayo-bound federal comparison ([cold food storage charts](https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/cold-food-storage-charts)). Opened commercial mayonnaise is a different product and a longer clock |
| With raw egg | Same day, or use pasteurized eggs | USDA FSIS advises against foods with raw or undercooked eggs and names Caesar salad and homemade mayonnaise ([Shell eggs from farm to table](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/eggs/shell-eggs-farm-table)). Cook them right away, or refrigerate and cook within 24 hours. Pasteurized in-shell eggs are the exception FSIS allows without cooking |
| Store-bought, opened | 1 to 3 months | University of Nebraska-Lincoln Extension lists commercial bottled dressing at 1 to 3 months in the refrigerator after opening, and the footnote treats it as a quality window ([Home Food Storage](https://food.unl.edu/free-resource/food-storage/), updated 2026). New Mexico State University Extension Guide E-118, revised April 2025, lists the same 1 to 3 months ([Storing Food Safely](https://pubs.nmsu.edu/_e/E118/index.html)). Iowa State University Extension says 1 to 3 months after opening ([condiment list](https://blogs.extension.iastate.edu/wellness/2019/05/21/refrigerated-condiment-safety-tips/)). Follow a shorter date if the label gives you one |

Worth being honest about the limits. No federal chart lists homemade salad dressing, so the homemade rows use the closest extension and USDA references. Read the vinaigrette, fresh garlic or herbs, dairy, mayo, and opened store-bought rows as quality windows. The raw-egg row is a safety limit. Garlic packed in oil with no acid is the other safety limit, and it isn't a row here because that jar isn't a dressing. University of Maine's basic card says 7 days even for a simple mix, so the 2-week line is the upper end Ask Extension gives a plain vinaigrette with dried herbs, not a dare.

The pattern is simple. More acid buys you time. More fresh, once-living ingredients takes it away. Oil, red wine vinegar, and dried oregano coasts for two weeks. A ranch with fresh dill and buttermilk is on a much shorter clock.

## How much sodium and sugar do homemade vinaigrettes save vs. bottled dressing, and what pH keeps them safe?

Italian dressing carries 150 mg sodium and 2 g sugar in one tablespoon (14.7 g, [FDC 171019](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/171019/nutrients)). A 3:1 olive-oil vinaigrette ([FDC 171413](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/171413/nutrients)) skips most of both. pH 4.6 or below inhibits *Clostridium botulinum* spores under [21 CFR 114](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-114) and the [Food Code](https://www.fda.gov/food/fda-food-code/food-code-2022). Garlic or herbs in plain oil sit near pH 5.3 to 6.7 and keep 4 days refrigerated.

The homemade spoon isn't the low-calorie one. It's the low-salt, low-sugar one. The servings below aren't matched, because USDA and the labels don't use the same spoon.

| Dressing | Calories | Sodium | Sugar | Typical pH tier | Fridge window |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|---|
| Homemade 3:1 olive oil and distilled vinegar, 1 tbsp | 90 kcal | under 1 mg | 0 g | Below 4.6. The water phase is vinegar | Up to 2 weeks if it's just oil, vinegar, and dried herbs |
| Commercial regular Italian, [FDC 171019](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/171019/nutrients), 1 tbsp (14.7 g) | 35 kcal | 146 mg (150 mg on a rounded label) | 1.6 g (2 g on a rounded label) | Acid dressing. This record doesn't publish a pH | 1 to 3 months after opening, or the date on the bottle |
| Hy-Vee classic Italian, [FDC 2066636](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/2066636/nutrients), 2 tbsp (31 g) | 100 kcal | 510 mg | 2 g | Vinegar is on the label. pH isn't in this record | 1 to 3 months after opening, or the date on the bottle |
| Sesame tahini dressing, [FDC 2164710](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/2164710/nutrients), 1 oz (30 g) | 110 kcal | 230 mg | 2 g | Rice vinegar and lemon juice are on the label. pH isn't in this record | 1 to 3 months after opening, or the date on the bottle |
| Yogurt or buttermilk ranch made at home. Numbers are commercial regular ranch, [FDC 173592](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173592/nutrients), 1 tbsp (15 g) | 65 kcal | 135 mg | 0.7 g | Dairy base. pH isn't in this record, so don't assume it's under 4.6 | 3 to 4 days for the homemade jar. Bottled ranch follows its label |

That 150 mg and 2 g are label rounding, not a second lab. FDC 171019 is 240 kcal, 993 mg sodium, and 10.77 g sugar per 100 g. Times 14.7 g, that's 35 kcal, 146 mg sodium, and 1.6 g sugar. Labels round sodium above 140 mg to the nearest 10 mg and sugar to the nearest gram.

The 3:1 tablespoon is three quarters of USDA's olive-oil tablespoon, 10.125 g from [FDC 171413](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/171413/nutrients), plus a quarter of the distilled-vinegar tablespoon, 3.725 g from [FDC 172237](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/172237/nutrients). That's 90 kcal, under 1 mg sodium, and 0 g sugar. You save the salt and the sugar. You don't save the olive oil. Whisking it yourself doesn't make the fat less caloric.

Hy-Vee's 510 mg and the tahini dressing's 230 mg are the printed label servings, 2 tablespoons and 1 ounce, not a quiet per-tablespoon conversion. The ranch tablespoon is commercial regular ranch: 15 g times 430 kcal, 901 mg sodium, and 4.69 g sugar per 100 g. That's 64.5 kcal before it rounds to 65, plus 135 mg sodium and 0.7 g sugar. A yogurt or buttermilk ranch you mix at home isn't that composite. It does share the 3-to-4-day clock from the first table. A bottled ranch with preservatives is the opened-dressing window, 1 to 3 months, unless the label is shorter.

The pH column is a tier, not a meter reading from your jar. [21 CFR 114](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-114) calls a food with a natural pH of 4.6 or below an acid food, and it requires acidified foods to finish at pH 4.6 or lower. Vinegar is that water phase in a plain vinaigrette. Garlic isn't. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources puts a raw clove around pH 5.3 to 6.3 ([preserving garlic](https://ucanr.edu/sites/default/files/2023-09/388534.pdf)). A University of Idaho study measured fresh basil, oregano, and rosemary at pH 6.3 to 6.7 before any acid soak ([Food Protection Trends, 2014](https://www.foodprotection.org/publications/food-protection-trends/archive/2014-07acidification-of-garlic-and-herbs-for-consumer-preparation-of-infused-oils/)). Pack those in oil with no vinegar and you're in the low-acid, no-oxygen case: refrigerate and use it within 4 days, or freeze it. If you can taste the vinegar in the same jar as the garlic, you're on the one-week fresh-ingredient window above, not the 4-day infused-oil clock. Lemon juice splashed in afterward still isn't the tested acidification.

## Do you have to refrigerate homemade salad dressing?

Anything with fresh produce, dairy, or egg in it, yes. That part isn't a preference. A plain oil, vinegar, and dried-herb vinaigrette is the one case where the perishable-food rules don't squarely apply, and we still keep it cold by default rather than sort out which jar is which. FDA advises keeping your refrigerator [at 40°F or below](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/refrigerator-thermometers-cold-facts-about-food-safety) and getting perishable food back in the cold within 2 hours. Oil keeps better cool and dark too. UC Davis recommends storing olive oil [in a cool, dark place](https://ucfoodquality.ucdavis.edu/olive-oil/olive-oil-myths-and-facts) to slow oxidation. Heat is a different job, and [which cooking oils belong in a hot pan](/cooking-oils-smoke-points-best-uses/) is the chart for the skillet, not the fridge.

One placement detail nobody mentions: USDA says [don't store perishable foods in the door](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/refrigeration), because door bin temperatures fluctuate more than the main cabinet. That door is exactly where most people park the dressing jar. Move it to a shelf.

Use glass while you're at it. Plastic absorbs odors and stains, and glass is non-reactive, which matters when the jar is half vinegar. A 16-ounce mason jar is the right size for a weekly batch.

## The raw garlic in oil rule you actually need to know

This is the one real safety issue here. [Oregon State University Extension](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/pub/sp-50-701-herbs-vegetables-oil) explains that low-acid foods stored in oil create "an oxygen-free environment which is perfect for growth" of *Clostridium botulinum*. The National Center for Home Food Preservation adds that [garlic-in-oil mixtures left at room temperature](https://nchfp.uga.edu/how/freeze/vegetable/freezing-garlic-in-oil/) are at risk for the development of botulism.

So the rule is short. Homemade flavored oils, including pesto and that trendy jar of garlic confit, go in the fridge and get used [within 4 days](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/pub/sp-50-701-herbs-vegetables-oil), or into the freezer for longer storage. Oregon State applies the same 4-day limit even when the garlic or herbs are dried, which surprises people. Label the container with the date you made it and the date four days later when it has to be used or frozen.

Commercial garlic-in-oil is either acidified during manufacturing or made with specific microbial inhibitors, which is why it can sit on a store shelf. Your kitchen isn't doing that, and splashing lemon juice into a jar of garlic and oil is not acidification. For a shelf-stable infused oil there is a tested Extension procedure, published as PNW 664, [Making Garlic- and Herb-Infused Oils at Home](https://catalog.extension.oregonstate.edu/pnw664). It covers a specific, limited set of herbs, and the directions have to be followed exactly. Read the publication itself before trying it, not a summary of it.

The line to watch is whether you've made a dressing or an infused oil. If you could pour it over a salad and taste the acid, it's a dressing, and Extension guidance puts fresh-ingredient dressings at one week. If it's basically garlic sitting in fat, it's an infused oil, and that caps at 4 days.

## Creamy dressings get the shortest quality window

Anything with mayo, sour cream, yogurt, or buttermilk is on a 3 to 4 day plan, borrowed from the federal listing for egg, chicken, ham, tuna, and macaroni salads. The two government pages disagree by a day, 3 to 4 on FoodSafety.gov versus 3 to 5 on USDA's refrigeration page, so take the shorter one.

One creamy dressing sits in its own category: a real Caesar or a homemade aioli is raw egg yolk. USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service [advises everyone against foods containing raw or undercooked eggs](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/eggs/shell-eggs-farm-table), naming Caesar salad and homemade mayonnaise specifically. FSIS says to cook dishes containing raw eggs immediately, or refrigerate and cook them within 24 hours, a much tighter clock than the 3 to 4 days above, and that assumes the dish gets cooked at all. The fix FSIS offers is pasteurized in-shell eggs or egg products, which "may be used safely without cooking", or heating the eggs with the liquid ingredients to 160°F before you combine everything. Want to skip the raw egg and the dairy clock in one move? A tahini-based vegan Caesar does both.

## Why your vinaigrette turns solid in the fridge

You'll pull the jar out and find a cloudy, semi-solid sludge. Don't throw it away. UC Davis says it's normal for olive oil [to get cloudy or to solidify in cold temperatures](https://ucfoodquality.ucdavis.edu/olive-oil/olive-oil-myths-and-facts) because of waxes naturally present in olives, and that this doesn't affect the oil's quality or safety. Cloudy and thick, though, not a solid brick.

What it doesn't do is prove you bought the good stuff. That "real olive oil solidifies in the fridge" trick gets repeated constantly, and UC Davis Olive Center testing found the [fridge test unreliable](https://www.ucdavis.edu/news/our-own-%E2%80%98dr-olive%E2%80%99-contradicts-dr-oz-%E2%80%98fridge-test%E2%80%99) for detecting either the purity or the quality of olive oil. That's why the test fails: their samples never fully solidified, even after 180 hours cold. Set the jar on the counter for 15 to 20 minutes, shake it hard, and pour.

## How to tell when dressing has gone bad

Here's the uncomfortable part. FDA warns that [food can make you very sick even when it doesn't look, smell, or taste spoiled](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/refrigerator-thermometers-cold-facts-about-food-safety), so your nose is a backup system, not the primary one. Write the date on the lid and go by that first.

That said, obvious signals still count. If the oil smells metallic, soapy, or like wet cardboard, it's gone rancid. Any fuzzy growth means the whole jar goes in the trash, not a scrape-and-save operation. USDA is direct about this for high-moisture foods: they [can be contaminated below the surface](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/molds-food-are-they-dangerous), and mold roots run deeper than the part you can see.

Separation is fine. We aren't adding xanthan gum here. A hard shake should bring it back. If it stays broken after that, the mustard or the dairy proteins have given up.
