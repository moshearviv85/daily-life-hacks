Two large eggs carry about 12.6 grams of protein. That's roughly 25 percent of the FDA's 50 gram Daily Value, and at $2.19 a dozen it cost about 37 cents. Eggs run 12.56 grams of protein per 100 grams of edible egg, so the arithmetic is simple: a large egg is around 50 grams, and each one brings about 6.3 grams.

Which means a two-egg breakfast is a solid quarter of your day's protein and nothing more than that. Here's the ladder.

| Eggs | Edible weight | Protein | Share of 50 g DV | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 egg | 50 g | 6.3 g | 13% | $0.18 |
| 2 eggs | 100 g | 12.6 g | 25% | $0.37 |
| 3 eggs | 150 g | 18.8 g | 38% | $0.55 |
| 4 eggs | 200 g | 25.1 g | 50% | $0.73 |
| 1 dozen | 600 g | 75.4 g | 151% | $2.19 |

*Source: USDA FoodData Central + US store prices, July 2026. Egg prices are the BLS US city average for May 2026. Full methodology at [/methodology/](/methodology/).*

## Is two eggs enough protein for a day?

No, and it's not close. Two eggs get you to about a quarter of the Daily Value, which leaves roughly 37 grams to find somewhere else.

That isn't a knock on eggs. It's just what a 100 gram portion of anything looks like against a whole day's target. Nobody expects a slice of toast to carry a day either. The problem only shows up when the two-egg breakfast is treated as the protein box being ticked, and then lunch is a salad and dinner is plain pasta, and the day quietly lands well short.

The fix is boring: give every meal a protein anchor rather than loading one meal. We went through what the daily target means and where it comes from in [where the 28g fiber and 50g protein targets come from](/fiber-protein-daily-values-explained/), and the personalized version is in [how much protein do you need per day](/how-much-protein-do-you-need-per-day/).

## Are eggs actually cheap protein?

Middling. This is the finding that annoys people the most, so let's be specific.

In our [protein per dollar study](/protein-per-dollar-cheapest-protein-sources/) across 49 foods, eggs landed 19th at 34.4 grams of protein per dollar. Every single dried legume beat them. Dry pinto beans returned 97.9, brown lentils 77.7, chickpeas 56.7. Whole wheat flour beat them at 96.0. Peanut butter beat them at 50.7, and so did chicken drumsticks at 50.3.

What eggs did beat is most of the rest of the store. Chicken breast came in at 24.5, ground beef 80/20 at 11.5, cheddar at 18.2, almonds at 14.8. Greek yogurt at 27.5 is the closest thing to a fair fight, and we ran that one properly in [eggs versus Greek yogurt](/eggs-vs-greek-yogurt-protein-cost/). The full picture of where eggs sit in the whole ranking is [are eggs the cheapest protein](/eggs-vs-everything-protein-value/).

So eggs are neither the bargain of the century nor a rip-off. They're a good middle-of-the-table option that happens to cook faster than anything else on the list, which is worth something no spreadsheet captures.

## What does a dozen eggs actually get you?

About 75.4 grams of protein, or one and a half Daily Values, for $2.19. That's the whole carton, 600 grams of edible egg.

Worth flagging where that price comes from. Most prices on this site are Walmart Great Value shelf tags, but eggs use the BLS US city average for May 2026, because egg prices swing hard and a single store tag on a single day isn't an honest national read. If your local carton is $4, halve every value in the table and the ranking shifts accordingly. Eggs are the one item in our dataset where the price you personally pay may differ wildly from ours.

## Do eggs have fiber?

Nothing worth counting. Eggs don't appear in our 53-food [fiber dataset](/fiber-per-dollar-cheapest-high-fiber-foods/) at all, because there's no fiber in them to rank.

That's the actual argument for what goes on the plate next to them. Two eggs plus a 60 gram bowl of rolled oats takes the meal from 12.6 grams of protein to about 20.5, and adds 6.1 grams of fiber that the eggs can't provide. [Savory oatmeal bowls with eggs](/savory-oatmeal-bowls-with-eggs-and-avocado/) is that combination done properly, and it sounds strange until you try it once.

Two slices of whole wheat toast add 6.5 grams of protein and 3.1 grams of fiber for about 18 cents, which is the cheapest upgrade available to an egg sandwich. Half a can of black beans adds 13.3 grams of protein and a serious fiber contribution for around 44 cents, and turns two eggs into [a breakfast hash](/sheet-pan-breakfast-hash-with-eggs-and-sweet-potatoes/) that actually holds until lunch.

## How to get the most out of a carton

Cook them in a way you'll repeat. The protein doesn't change based on technique, so the only question is whether the eggs get eaten or go grey in the back of the fridge.

If breakfast is the weak point of your day, building the plate around a protein and a fiber source at the same time is the highest-leverage change available. [A balanced breakfast that keeps you full](/balanced-breakfast-that-keeps-you-full/) walks through it, and the cost side is ranked in [the cheapest high-protein breakfast foods](/breakfast-staples-per-dollar/).

Two eggs is 12.6 grams and 37 cents. Know the number, stop assuming it's more, and build the rest of the plate accordingly. Where eggs fit against everything else on a tight budget is mapped out in [our high protein on a budget guide](/high-protein-on-a-budget-complete-guide/).
