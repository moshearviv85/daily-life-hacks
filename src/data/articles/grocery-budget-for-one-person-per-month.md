USDA's thrifty benchmark puts one adult at roughly $303 to $381 a month for food eaten at home, depending on whether the adult is a woman or a man. That range comes from May 2026 prices, and it already includes the 20 percent surcharge USDA applies to people who shop for a household of one.

That surcharge is the part worth understanding, because it explains why your receipt looks unreasonable next to your friend's family-of-four grocery stories.

| USDA thrifty figure, one adult | Weekly | Monthly | Per day |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adult man, in a family | $73.20 | $317.16 | $10.46 |
| Adult woman, in a family | $58.20 | $252.17 | $8.31 |
| Adult man, living alone (+20%) | $87.84 | $380.59 | $12.55 |
| Adult woman, living alone (+20%) | $69.84 | $302.60 | $9.98 |

*Source: USDA Food and Nutrition Service, Official USDA Thrifty Food Plan, U.S. Average, May 2026 ([published report](https://www.fns.usda.gov/sites/default/files/resource-files/cnpp-costfood-tfp-may2026.pdf)). Monthly figures use USDA's own weekly-to-monthly ratio. Full methodology at [/methodology/](/methodology/).*

## Where does the one-person number come from?

USDA doesn't publish a headline "single person" figure, which is why this question is so hard to answer with a straight face on most budgeting sites. What it publishes is a cost per person by age and sex, plus a household-size adjustment.

The individual weekly costs for May 2026 are $73.20 for an adult man aged 20 to 50 and $58.20 for an adult woman in the same band. Then USDA says to add 20 percent for a one-person household, 10 percent for two people, and 5 percent for three. Four people get no adjustment, and households of five or six subtract 5 percent.

Run that and you get $87.84 and $69.84 a week. Convert with USDA's own weekly-to-monthly ratio, the same one that turns its $235.00 family week into a $1,018.20 family month, and you land at about $381 and $303. We walked through the family version of these numbers in [what USDA says a grocery budget for a family of four costs](/usda-thrifty-food-plan-weekly-cost/).

## Why does shopping for one cost more per person?

Because groceries are sold in family-shaped packages and you are not a family.

The cheapest food in the store is also the food sold in the biggest bags. A four pound bag of dry pinto beans at $3.97 is the single best protein value we've priced, at 97.9 grams of protein per dollar, and it's also 1,814 grams of beans that one person has to work through before they get bored. A 40 ounce jar of peanut butter at $4.97 is a better deal per ounce than the small jar every single time, and it's still a lot of peanut butter.

That's the whole 20 percent. Smaller packages, more waste, less ability to buy the loss-leader case of something. USDA priced the penalty; it didn't invent it.

The good news is the penalty is dodgeable in specific places. Shelf-stable food doesn't spoil, so buying the big bag of rice or flour costs you nothing but cupboard space. The 20 percent bites hardest on fresh produce, dairy, and meat, which is where a single shopper genuinely does throw more away. Cutting that waste is the highest-value habit available, and [how to reduce food waste at home](/how-to-reduce-food-waste-at-home-easy-tips/) is the practical version.

## What does $300 a month actually buy?

More than most people expect, if the money goes to the right shelves.

Our [protein per dollar study](/protein-per-dollar-cheapest-protein-sources/) puts 50 grams of protein, the full FDA Daily Value, at about $0.51 when it comes from dry pinto beans. The [fiber per dollar ranking](/fiber-per-dollar-cheapest-high-fiber-foods/) puts 28 grams of fiber at about $0.36 from whole wheat flour. Those two numbers are not a diet and I'm not suggesting anyone eat that way, but they set the floor. The nutrients themselves are almost free. Everything above the floor is variety, convenience, and the fact that you're a person and not a spreadsheet.

Between the floor and the benchmark there's a lot of room. [What a day of 50 grams of protein costs](/what-50-grams-of-protein-costs-per-day/) prices five realistic versions of the same day, from 82 cents to $9.97, and the expensive one is not more nourishing. It's just bought differently.

## How do you actually hit the number?

Build a small rotation and shop it, rather than shopping by mood and hoping. Cooking for one is the format most recipes ignore, so a plan matters more than it does for a household where leftovers get eaten by somebody else.

Three habits do most of the work. Buy shelf-stable staples in the largest package you'll finish. Cook in batches and freeze in single portions, which is the entire premise of [meal prepping on a budget for one person](/how-to-meal-prep-on-a-budget-for-one-person/). And give every perishable at least two planned meals before it goes in the cart, a rule we lean on in [the budget grocery list](/grocery-shopping-list-for-healthy-eating-on-a-budget/).

If you want ideas that are actually sized for one, [budget meal ideas for one](/budget-meal-ideas-for-one/) is written for that problem specifically.

## What the benchmark leaves out

Two things, and they're the reason people conclude they're failing when they aren't.

First, USDA's number is food at home. No restaurants, no delivery fees, no coffee bought because the morning went sideways. If your $400 includes three takeout dinners, you are not overspending on groceries. You are spending on something else that happens to arrive on the same card statement.

Second, it isn't your zip code. USDA reports a national average, and our own prices come from one store in one month, July 2026. Both are useful as a shape. Neither is a quote. If a cheaper store is a drive away, [we did the math on whether that drive pays](/is-driving-to-cheaper-grocery-store-worth-it/).

Use the $303 to $381 range the way you'd use a bathroom scale rather than a report card: a number to compare against yourself over time. The full system for pulling it down without eating badly is in [the eat healthy on a budget playbook](/eat-healthy-on-a-budget-complete-playbook/).
