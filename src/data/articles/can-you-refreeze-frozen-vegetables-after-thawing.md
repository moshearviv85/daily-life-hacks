If the bag thawed in the refrigerator, you can refreeze it. If it thawed on the counter for more than two hours, you can't. That's the whole rule, and it comes straight from USDA's [freezing and food safety guidance](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/freezing-and-food-safety), which draws the line by where the food sat rather than by how it looks or smells.

I know the temptation. The peas look fine. They're still cold. Cold is not the standard.

| Situation | Safe to refreeze? | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Thawed in the refrigerator | Yes, with quality loss | [USDA FSIS](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/freezing-and-food-safety) |
| Left out of refrigeration over 2 hours | No | USDA FSIS |
| Left out over 1 hour above 90F | No | USDA FSIS |
| Thawed, then cooked | Yes, freeze it as the cooked dish | USDA FSIS |
| Still has ice crystals after a power outage | Yes | USDA FSIS |
| Warmer than 40F for more than 2 hours | No, discard | USDA FSIS |

*Source: USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service, Freezing and Food Safety, last updated August 2024.*

## Can you refreeze vegetables that thawed in the fridge?

Yes, and FSIS says so in one sentence: once food is thawed in the refrigerator, it is safe to refreeze it without cooking, although there may be a loss of quality due to the moisture lost through thawing.

Notice what's doing the work there. The refrigerator held the vegetables below the temperature where bacteria get busy the whole time they were thawing. Nothing had a chance to multiply, so nothing changed about the safety picture.

The extension pages are pickier about texture, and one of them is pickier about the refreeze itself. [FoodSafety.gov's power-outage chart](https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/food-safety-during-power-outage) says home or commercially packaged vegetables can be refrozen if they still contain ice crystals or are at 40 degrees F or below, and should be discarded after they've been held above 40 degrees F for 6 hours. [NDSU](https://www.ndsu.edu/agriculture/extension/publications/food-freezing-basics-packaging-loading-freezer-and-refreezing) says vegetables should be refrozen only if they still contain plenty of ice crystals, and that food still below 40 degrees F can be refrozen. [University of Minnesota Extension](https://extension.umn.edu/food/preparing/cooking-at-home/power-outages-keep-food-safe) says don't refreeze thawed vegetables, and that foods which still contain ice crystals can be refrozen. The practical line is the strict one: refreeze a bag that thawed in the fridge and is still at or below 40 degrees F, ideally while it still has ice crystals. A bag that sat on the counter is not that bag.

The quality picture did change, and we'll get to why.

## How long can frozen vegetables sit out?

Two hours. One hour if it's above 90F, which covers a hot kitchen, a car, or a picnic table.

FSIS states the limit directly in the refreezing section: do not refreeze any foods left outside the refrigerator longer than 2 hours, and 1 hour in temperatures above 90F. The [danger zone guidance](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/danger-zone-40f-140f) explains why the window is that tight. Bacteria grow most rapidly between 40F and 140F, "doubling in number in as little as 20 minutes."

Twenty minutes. That's a school pickup. This is why the grocery bag that sat in the trunk while you ran one more errand is a real question and not a paranoid one.

## What if the power went out?

Then you're grading the freezer, not the clock. FSIS says a full freezer will usually keep about 2 days with the door kept shut, and a half-full freezer about a day.

When power returns, the test is physical: if food is partly frozen, still has ice crystals, or is as cold as if it were in a refrigerator at 40F, it's safe to refreeze or use. Discard foods that have been warmer than 40F for more than 2 hours, and discard anything contaminated by raw meat juices.

FSIS also warns against the move everyone reaches for in winter, which is setting food outside in the snow. Sun exposure can thaw frozen food even in cold air, the outdoor temperature swings hour to hour, and animals get involved. Dry ice or a friend's freezer is the recommendation instead.

## Why do refrozen vegetables taste worse?

Because of what the ice does on the way in and out.

FSIS explains that rapid freezing keeps ice crystals small, while slow freezing creates large, disruptive crystals that damage cells during thawing and cause food to drip and lose juiciness. Commercial freezing is fast. Your home freezer, working on a bag that's already thawed and full of liquid water, is not.

So a refrozen bag of green beans is safe and softer. That's a soup ingredient now, not a sheet pan ingredient. Push it into a stew, a [rice and beans skillet](/quick-20-minute-high-fiber-meals-for-busy-days/), or anything with a sauce, and nobody will file a complaint.

If you'd rather not deal with the question at all, cook the thawed vegetables and freeze the finished dish. FSIS says after cooking raw foods which were previously frozen, it is safe to freeze the cooked foods, which is the whole logic behind [freezer meal prep](/freezer-meal-prep-ideas-for-beginners/).

## How long frozen vegetables last

Safety and quality are not the same clock. [FoodSafety.gov](https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/cold-food-storage-charts) says food held continuously at 0 degrees F stays safe indefinitely, and that the times on a freezer chart are about quality. For eating quality, the [National Center for Home Food Preservation](https://nchfp.uga.edu/how/freeze/general-freezing-information/how-long-can-i-store-frozen-foods/) puts fruits and vegetables at roughly 8 to 12 months. [Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/food-safety/freezer-storage) lists commercially frozen vegetables at about 8 months for top quality. Past that, the bag can taste like the freezer. Freezer burn is a dry spot, not a safety verdict. Cut it off if the rest still smells like vegetables.

A best-by date on the bag is a quality date. If the freezer can't hold 0 degrees F, or the door is open all evening, eat those bags sooner. An appliance thermometer between the packages is the only way to know which freezer you actually own.

## Do you have to cook frozen vegetables

Yes, unless the package says ready to eat. The factory step is blanching, which the [National Center for Home Food Preservation](https://nchfp.uga.edu/how/freeze/general-freezing-information/blanching-vegetables/) defines as a short scald in boiling water or steam to stop the enzymes that wreck color, flavor, and texture. Their table is minutes, not a meal: 3 minutes for green beans, 1.5 minutes for green peas, 2 minutes for sliced carrots. [University of Minnesota Extension](https://extension.umn.edu/preserving-and-preparing/how-blanch-vegetables-safe-preservation) adds that blanching also kills some bacteria on the surface. It is still not dinner.

[Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/be-smart-eat-well-get-healthy/2017-04-11-cook-frozen-vegetables), writing after frozen-vegetable recalls, says to cook the vegetables before they go into a cold salad. Listeria is killed by heat, and heating food to 155 degrees F will kill the bacteria. Freezing to 0 degrees F inactivates microbes, [USDA FSIS says](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/freezing-and-food-safety), and once the food thaws those microbes can wake up. Follow the bag when it gives you a time. Stirring a raw handful into a cold pasta salad is the move to skip.

## How to cook them without the mush

Cook them frozen. [NDSU Extension](https://www.ndsu.edu/agriculture/extension/publications/freezing-vegetables) says most frozen vegetables should be cooked without thawing first. Corn on the cob is the exception they name, because the cob needs a partial thaw to heat through. Thawing anything else first just means the vegetables sit in their own meltwater.

They were partly cooked when they were blanched, so they need less time than fresh. A hot sheet pan, a single layer, and no lid. Crowding or covering turns the ice into steam and the steam into boiled vegetables. Broccoli, cauliflower, green beans, and Brussels sprouts can take that heat. Peas and spinach are too wet to brown. Stir those into a sauce instead of asking them for a crust. Pull them when a fork goes in, not when you've forgotten the pan.

## How to freeze fresh vegetables

Blanch, chill, dry, freeze flat. NCHFP calls blanching a must for almost all vegetables you plan to freeze, because freezing slows enzymes and doesn't stop them. Underblanching, they warn, stimulates enzyme activity and is worse than no blanching at all. Both NCHFP and NDSU say skip the microwave for this. It doesn't reliably knock the enzymes out.

University of Minnesota Extension's exceptions, the ones that don't need a blanch, are diced or cut onions, green onions, leeks, and peppers. Times from the NCHFP table, in boiling water unless noted: green beans 3 minutes, green peas 1.5, diced or sliced carrots 2, cauliflower florets 3, broccoli 3 in water or 5 in steam. Then ice water until they're cold, drain until the surface isn't wet, and freeze in a single layer before you stack the bags. Wet bags become one vegetable brick.

NDSU's poor candidates for the freezer are the ones you wanted raw and crisp: cabbage, green onions for garnish, lettuce and other salad greens, radishes, cucumbers, and tomatoes unless you're freezing them for cooking. Home-frozen vegetables sit in that same 8 to 12 month quality window.

For the underlying temperatures, food storage temperature rules covers the 40F and 0F targets that make all of this work. If it's cooked food rather than a vegetable bag you're weighing up, how long leftovers last is the one you want.
