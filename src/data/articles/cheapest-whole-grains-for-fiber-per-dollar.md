Quinoa gets the nice bag. Barley gets the receipt. I wanted a ranking of grains you can actually cook, set next to the flour you'd bake and the loaf someone else already baked, because "eat more whole grains" doesn't name a price.

The [Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020-2025](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/sites/default/files/2020-12/Dietary_Guidelines_for_Americans_2020-2025.pdf) say to make half your grains whole. They don't tell you which half is the cheap fiber. This page does, from grains-fiber-per-dollar-ranked-2026.csv. The filename stays in the sentence. This page doesn't link the file.

## Which cookable whole grains give you the most fiber per dollar?

Pearled barley. Dry, it returns 57.1 grams of fiber per dollar ([FDC 170284](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170284/nutrients)). Whole wheat bread returns 9.7 grams ([FDC 172688](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/172688/nutrients)). Divide 57.1 by 9.7 and you get about 5.9. That's the spread between the top cookable grain and the loaf. Whole wheat flour is higher still, at 77.8 grams, and you don't eat it with a spoon. Popcorn prints 51.3 grams in the same file and stays off the verified list.

The prices are the July 2026 grocery study described on the [methodology page](/methodology/), with one dated exception. Whole wheat bread is the May 2026 BLS average, $3.52 for a 20 oz loaf, because that's the last month BLS published for that series. The other packages are the Walmart store-checks already in the grain file, and those lines don't carry their own month. Your receipt can disagree. That's the deal with a national snapshot.

Among the foods you can simmer, boil, or sauce, the verified order is barley, then oats, then whole wheat spaghetti. Bran flakes sit between the spaghetti and the bulgur on the dollar ranking, and they don't join that sentence. FDC 173888 is a close proxy, not a verified winner.

## Flour wins, then you have to cook it

Whole wheat flour returns 77.8 grams of fiber per dollar, from 10.7 grams per 100 grams ([FDC 168893](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168893/nutrients)). A 5 pound bag was $3.12. Best math on the chart. Useless dinner if the bag stays shut.

Pearled barley is the first row you can put in a pot and call dinner. Oats and whole wheat spaghetti follow it, and the pasta doesn't look like a "whole grain breakfast." It still beats the rice. If beans are the part of the fiber plan you want to skip, the same prices show up in a [30 gram day without beans](/high-fiber-without-beans/). This page is the grain aisle alone, flour and bread included.

Bulgur and oat bran wear the same $3.49 sticker. The bran bag is smaller, 18 ounces against 24, so the bran returns less fiber per dollar even though each 100 grams is denser. Brown rice is the cheap familiar bag, 3.6 grams of fiber per 100 grams ([FDC 169703](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/169703/nutrients)), and it still isn't winning. Once the pot is cooked, the dollar ranking is done and the fridge clock starts. That's [how long cooked grains last in the fridge](/how-long-do-cooked-grains-and-beans-last-in-the-fridge/).

## Popcorn stays out of the verified winners

Popcorn kernels show 51.3 grams of fiber per dollar, a 2 pound bag at $2.28. That would sit between barley and oats if I let it. I don't.

The grain file leaves the USDA id blank. The fiber file calls the row unresolved. USDA publishes popped popcorn, not a kernel record, so the 12.9 grams per 100 grams is derived from air-popped popcorn, [FDC 167959](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/167959/nutrients), using the moisture gap between kernels and popped corn. Treat 51.3 as our math. It isn't a USDA figure, and it isn't a verified winner on this page. The chart has ten bars. Popcorn isn't one of them.

Bran flakes are the other row I won't let anyone quote as a clean win. 30.1 grams per dollar looks tidy. [FDC 173888](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173888/nutrients) is POST Bran Flakes, 18.3 grams of fiber per 100 grams, and the priced box was a 17.3 oz store brand at $2.98. No generic bran-flakes record exists to swap in. Close proxy. In the table. Out of the winner sentence.

## The loaf is the wheat after someone else baked it

100% whole wheat bread is 9.7 grams of fiber per dollar. Six grams per 100 grams. A 20 oz loaf at $3.52. The 5.9 times gap is barley versus that loaf, for someone who wants a grain they can cook. Flour against the same loaf is a wider gap, and it isn't the 5.9, because flour still needs a recipe. Bread is convenient. Convenience is the $3.52.

Quinoa is how you spend the grain budget and feel organized. A 2 pound bag was $5.97, 10.6 grams per dollar ([FDC 168874](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168874/nutrients)). It beats the loaf by a little and loses to barley by a lot. Buy it because you want quinoa. Don't ask it to be the thrifty fiber plan.

The full fiber table, produce included, is the [fiber per dollar calculator](/tools/fiber-per-dollar-calculator/). Fruit and vegetables have their own cut on the [cheapest high-fiber produce page](/cheapest-high-fiber-fruits-and-vegetables/). Grains were the piece that ranking never pulled out on their own.

## What I'd buy when the goal is fiber per dollar

A bag of pearled barley, the oats I already eat, and a box of whole wheat spaghetti. That's the verified cookable top. The flour stays for a baking day. The loaf stays for the morning I won't cook. Quinoa stays on the shelf until I actually want it.

Half the grains on the plate can be whole. The guideline said that. It didn't say they have to be the expensive ones. Barley at 57.1 grams per dollar is the cookable grain that makes the half cheaper.
