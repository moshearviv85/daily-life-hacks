I used to put away a family-sized bag of sour cream and onion chips before the opening credits were over. It was never about hunger. It was the crunch and the salt that stays on your fingers for three days.

Here's the short version. Per ounce, USDA FoodData Central puts [plain salted potato chips](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/169677/nutrients) at 149 calories, 0.9 gram of fiber and 148 mg of sodium. [Air-popped popcorn](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/167959/nutrients) at the same weight is 108 calories, 4.1 grams of fiber and 2 mg of sodium. That swap moves every number in the same direction at once. Most of the other options in the "better chip" aisle move a lot less than the bag implies.

Healthy alternatives to potato chips for snacking don't have to mean raw celery. You need salt, fat and crunch. If a snack doesn't make enough noise to annoy someone in the next room, it won't satisfy a chip craving.

## What actually changes when you swap chips?

Everything below is one ounce, 28 grams, the serving size on a standard chip bag. Same weight, same column, no snack algebra.

| Snack, 1 ounce (28 g) | Calories | Fiber | Sodium |
|---|---|---|---|
| Potato chips, plain, salted (the baseline) | 149 | 0.9 g | 148 mg |
| Air-popped popcorn | 108 | 4.1 g | 2 mg |
| Almonds, dry roasted, salted | 167 | 3.1 g | 66 mg |
| Sunflower kernels, dry roasted, salted | 163 | 2.5 g | 183 mg |
| Pumpkin seed kernels, roasted, salted | 161 | 1.8 g | 72 mg |
| Sweet potato chips, fried, unsalted | 149 | 2.5 g | 10 mg |
| Kale chips, one brand (label data) | 120 | 3.0 g | 253 mg |
| Store veggie chips (label data) | 140 | 1.0 g | 260 mg |
| Veggie straws (label data) | 129 | 0 g | 197 mg |
| Chickpeas, cooked, weighed before roasting | 46 | 2.1 g | 2 mg |
| Cucumber, raw, with peel | 4 | 0.1 g | under 1 mg |

All values are USDA FoodData Central per-100-gram figures converted to 28 grams: [chips](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/169677/nutrients), [popcorn](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/167959/nutrients), [almonds](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168596/nutrients), [sunflower kernels](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/169418/nutrients), [pumpkin seeds](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/169415/nutrients), [sweet potato chips](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/174783/nutrients), [kale chips](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/2042741/nutrients), [veggie chips](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/2064843/nutrients), [veggie straws](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/1876680/nutrients), [chickpeas](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173757/nutrients), [cucumber](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168409/nutrients). The kale and the two veggie rows are Branded Foods records, so those numbers come off an older manufacturer label rather than a lab, and other brands will read differently. Flavored chips, salted sweet potato chips and buttered popcorn all shift things.

For scale, the [FDA Daily Value for dietary fiber is 28 grams](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/daily-value-nutrition-and-supplement-facts-labels) and for sodium it's 2,300 mg. So an ounce of popcorn is about 15 percent of a day's fiber, an ounce of chips about 3 percent, and that ounce of store veggie chips about 11 percent of the day's sodium. Our deeper [popcorn vs potato chips fiber comparison](/popcorn-vs-potato-chips-fiber-comparison/) has the receipts on that one matchup.

## Why is air-popped popcorn the default swap?

Volume. USDA weighs a cup of air-popped popcorn at 8 grams, so three cups is 24 grams: about 93 calories and roughly 3.5 grams of fiber. USDA counts 22 chips in a 28 gram ounce, so those three cups cost you about what 14 chips cost. Fourteen chips is a handful. Three cups is a bowl you can dig around in for twenty minutes.

One small trial is worth knowing about. In a 2012 *Nutrition Journal* study of 35 normal-weight adults, [six cups of popcorn produced greater satiety ratings than one cup of potato chips](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3502142/), and total intake across the snack plus the lunch that followed came out lower for popcorn, 698 to 739 calories against 803, even though the popcorn was 100 calories and the chips were 150. The lunch itself was near identical, 639 calories after popcorn and 653 after chips, so the gap comes from the snack and not from the meal after it. The honest caveat: ConAgra Foods, which makes Orville Redenbacher and Act II popcorn, funded it, and one author worked there. A single-session study paid for by the popcorn industry isn't proof of anything. It's a hint that lines up with the volume math.

Skip the movie theater butter in those questionable plastic jugs. A light mist of olive oil gets seasonings to stick, and nutritional yeast tastes vaguely like cheese without leaving you greasy. These [healthy high fiber popcorn toppings](/high-fiber-popcorn-toppings-healthy/) go well beyond the salt shaker.

## Are veggie chips and sweet potato chips actually better?

This is where the aisle gets you. The store brand veggie chips in USDA's Branded Foods data list potato flour and potato starch as ingredients one and two, with spinach powder, tomato paste and beetroot powder at the end doing color work. One ounce: 140 calories, 1 gram of fiber, 260 mg of sodium. That's more sodium than the plain chips you were avoiding. The veggie straws entry is worse on fiber, listing potato starch first and 0 gram per serving.

Kale chips are the swap everyone names first, and USDA has no lab entry for them at all, only a hundred-plus manufacturer records in Branded Foods. One of those, a dehydrated organic brand, runs 120 calories, 3 grams of fiber and 253 mg of sodium per ounce. The fiber is real and it beats every other chip in the table. The calories land only 29 below plain chips and the sodium lands well above them, because the crunch is coming from sunflower seeds and tahini, not from the leaf.

Sweet potato chips do better, but not the way people assume. The unsalted USDA entry carries 2.5 grams of fiber per ounce, nearly triple plain chips, and the exact same 149 calories, because it's still a starchy slice fried in oil. Buying them for the fiber is a real gain. Buying them for the calories is a wash.

Making your own is the better move if you own a mandoline. Slice sweet potatoes or beets paper-thin, toss with a little oil, bake at 300 degrees Fahrenheit, and expect it to take a while, because you're basically dehydrating them.

## Do roasted chickpeas actually beat chips?

On fiber and protein, yes, and it isn't close. Half a cup of cooked chickpeas, 82 grams, is about 134 calories, 6.2 grams of fiber and 7.3 grams of protein before anything hits the oven. That's around seven times the fiber of an ounce of chips for fewer calories. Roasting removes water and adds whatever oil and salt you use, so the honest measurement happens before the pan, not after.

**Pro tip:** dry them thoroughly with a paper towel first. If they're even a little damp they steam instead of crisping, which is a tragedy. Roast at 400 degrees Fahrenheit for 25 to 35 minutes until golden, then let them cool, because they crisp more as they sit. Our [crispy roasted chickpeas recipe](/crispy-roasted-chickpeas-high-fiber-snack/) nails the texture, and it finally uses that can of garbanzo beans that's been in your pantry since the Obama administration.

## Which nuts and seeds are worth it, and which are saltier than chips?

Sometimes the chip craving is just a salt craving, and a handful of almonds shuts it down faster than a bag of Lay's. Salted dry roasted almonds run 167 calories an ounce, more than chips, with 3.1 grams of fiber and 66 mg of sodium. You're trading calories for fiber and a snack that's harder to inhale by the fistful.

Watch the seeds, though. Dry roasted salted sunflower kernels carry 183 mg of sodium per ounce, more than the 148 mg in the chips you're replacing. Roasted salted pumpkin seeds are gentler at 72 mg, but their 1.8 grams of fiber per ounce is less than half what the same weight of popcorn brings. Buy the unsalted version and salt the bowl yourself. It's cheaper too, and our [high-fiber snacks per dollar ranking](/high-fiber-snacks-per-dollar/) compares popcorn, chickpeas, nuts and the rest of the 3 PM suspects.

Sunflower seeds still in the shell are my pick when you need to keep your hands busy. Getting to the seed takes effort, which blocks the mindless shoveling a bowl of chips invites.

## What if you just want something cold and crisp?

Slice a cucumber thin, hit it with salt and a splash of vinegar, and it does a passable impression of a salt and vinegar chip for about 4 calories an ounce. It's 95 percent water, so its 0.1 gram of fiber per ounce isn't doing anything for your daily total and it won't hold you for six hours. It's there for the crunch and the cold, which is sometimes the entire point. Sliced radishes work the same way with a sharper bite. Neither one snaps like a fried chip unless you dehydrate it.

You don't have to swear off potatoes forever to make a better call at 3:00 PM. Trading one ounce of chips for three cups of air-popped popcorn is about 56 fewer calories, roughly 146 mg less sodium and about 2.6 grams more fiber, and it takes four minutes. Pick one swap, keep it in the pantry, and stop letting the vending machine decide how your afternoon goes.
