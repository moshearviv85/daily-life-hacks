Can you use sourdough discard from a new starter? Not yet. [King Arthur's discard guide](https://www.kingarthurbaking.com/blog/2024/04/18/sourdough-discard) says not to start saving discard until the starter is really up and running. If you've just begun and it isn't established, you shouldn't keep that discard.

The [beginner discard recipes](/easy-sourdough-discard-recipes-beginners/) on this site use the same line in shorter form. They want an established starter that rises predictably after feeding. This page is the longer version of that answer, including the day-by-day questions people actually type.

## Can you use sourdough discard from a new starter?

Don't bake with it, and don't park it in a fridge jar, until the starter reliably becomes very bubbly and doubles in size within 6 to 8 hours of feeding. That's [King Arthur's ready test](https://www.kingarthurbaking.com/learn/guides/sourdough/create). They say it could be 7 days or less after you begin, or up to a couple of weeks. Watch the jar, not the calendar.

[The FDA advises against tasting raw flour, dough, or batter](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/raw-doughs-raw-deal-and-could-make-you-sick). A tiny taste isn't how you check whether day 4 is "ready." Cooked recipes come later. Raw batter doesn't get a sample spoon.

## Why the first days of discard are not the same ingredient

On day 1, King Arthur has you stir whole wheat or pumpernickel flour with water and wait. On day 2 you discard about half and feed what's left. By day 3 you're discarding down to 113 grams, twice a day, because the mixture is still becoming a starter. The portion that leaves the jar in that stretch isn't the unfed starter their discard recipes mean.

Pantry Mama tells readers to toss discard for at least the first 7 days, and she writes that early discard can contain bad bacteria. That's her published minimum. It doesn't replace King Arthur's rise test, and it doesn't make day 7 a promise. If the jar still isn't doubling on a schedule, you're in the "keep feeding" stretch, even if a week has passed.

## How to tell your starter is established

King Arthur's visible test, the one you can check without tasting anything:

- It reliably doubles in the container within 6 to 8 hours of a feeding.
- It's very bubbly, including the finer bubbles they describe on the surface.
- The rise is consistent, not a one-time puff you never see again.

A clear, straight-sided jar helps because you can see the height. This site's wording for the same idea is plainer: a starter that rises predictably after feeding. If it doesn't do that yet, it isn't the ingredient in the [gram-by-gram discard list](/what-to-make-with-sourdough-discard-by-amount/).

The [discard conversion guide](/how-to-measure-sourdough-discard-grams/) is for weighing that later jar. A more precise weight doesn't turn day 2 into discard you can cook.

## What to do with the early discard instead

Throw away the portion you remove. Keep feeding the part that stays in the jar, on King Arthur's schedule, until the rise test shows up. That's the whole job. Don't bake the early removals, and don't taste them.

Once the starter is established, routine discard is a different pile. King Arthur says you can then keep it and bake with it. Until then, the removed mixture isn't a recipe waiting to happen. It's the part you took out so the rest has enough food.

## Cold or old discard from an established starter is fine

Cold isn't the same problem as new. After the starter is healthy and active, King Arthur says to keep discard from ordinary feedings, in the fridge if you aren't baking that day. The beginner recipes use that cold jar. They don't ask you to warm it up or feed it first, because baking soda or the oven is doing the work, not a fresh rise.

Old is a storage question, and the published clocks don't agree. [How long that fridge jar lasts](/how-long-does-sourdough-discard-last/), and when freezing is the cleaner plan, is the page for the date on the lid. A young starter and a neglected jar fail in different ways. Don't mix the two up and toss a good jar, or bake a bad one, because the internet used one word for both.

## Hooch, acetone smell, and pink or orange streaks

King Arthur's throw-out list is specific. Visible mold, an orange or pink tint or streak, or a putrid smell means throw it out and start over. A dark liquid on top is hooch. They say to stir that back in and feed a hungry starter. Their discard article says the same about black liquid, and the same about pink or orange streaks.

Acetone is the argument. King Arthur says a starter should smell sharply sour but clean, without an oddly chemical smell like acetone. That sentence is about aroma, and it isn't the same sentence as the throw-out list. Pantry Mama says discard that's a few days old can smell acidic or like acetone and still be usable, as long as there's no mold and no pink or orange.

So the decision is the overlap, not the fight. Mold, pink, orange, or a rotten smell means the jar goes out. An acetone smell means the culture is very hungry and the sources don't give you one rule. If you wouldn't want that smell in the pancakes, throw it out. [Discard versus a ripe starter](/sourdough-discard-vs-active-starter/) is about feeding time and lift, not about rescuing a jar that looks wrong.

## Your first discard recipe once the starter is ready

When the rise is reliable, the first cooks are the small ones. Half a cup, about 113 grams, is one skillet flatbread. A full cup, about 227 grams, is pancakes, crackers, or the thin pizza crust. Those are the [beginner discard recipes](/easy-sourdough-discard-recipes-beginners/), and they assume 100% hydration discard from the established jar. Weigh it. Then cook it through.
