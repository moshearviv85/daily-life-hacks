A high-fiber yogurt parfait is a bookkeeping problem more than a recipe problem. Plain Greek yogurt carries no fiber at all, so every gram comes from the berries, the seeds, and the granola. Build it one way and you're at 13 grams before you sit down. Build it another way, in a bowl that photographs identically, and you're at 5.

That gap is the whole article. Most parfait recipes quote a single fiber number and then hand you a granola whose label serving is 1/4 cup, which is not the amount anyone pours.

If granola's joining the jar, use this [guide to choosing granola that isn't dessert](/how-to-choose-granola-not-dessert/) before breakfast quietly turns into a cookie with yogurt underneath it.

## How much fiber is in a yogurt parfait?

| Layer | USDA fiber per 100 g | Amount | Fiber |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plain Greek yogurt, whole milk | 0.0 g | 1 cup | 0 g |
| Raspberries, fresh | 6.5 g | 1/2 cup (62 g) | 4.0 g |
| Blackberries, fresh | 5.3 g | 1/2 cup (72 g) | 3.8 g |
| Chia seeds | 34.4 g | 1 tbsp (12 g, kitchen measure) | 4.1 g |
| Raspberries, frozen unsweetened | 4.3 g | 1/2 cup (70 g) | 3.0 g |
| Granola, USDA survey average | 7.4 g | 1/2 cup (55 g) | 4.1 g |
| Ground flaxseed | 27.3 g | 1 tbsp (7 g) | 1.9 g |
| Blueberries, fresh | 2.4 g | 1/2 cup (74 g) | 1.8 g |
| Strawberries, halved | 2.0 g | 1/2 cup (76 g) | 1.5 g |

Fiber per 100 grams and the cup weights both come from [USDA FoodData Central](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-search/), records 171304, 167755, 173946, 170554, 168209, 2708461, 169414, 171711 and 167762, checked 2026-07-31. Half-cup weights are USDA's own one-cup weights halved. USDA gives ground flaxseed a tablespoon weight directly, 7 grams. For chia it publishes only a 1-ounce portion, 28.35 grams, and no spoon or cup measure at all. The 12 grams in the chia row is the standard kitchen tablespoon conversion, not a USDA portion, and it is flagged in the table for that reason. USDA's own ounce of chia carries 9.8 grams of fiber if you want the figure that is fully sourced.

Now add three of them up. Raspberries plus chia plus average granola is 12.2 grams, about 44 percent of the FDA's [28-gram Daily Value for fiber](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/daily-value-nutrition-and-supplement-facts-labels). Strawberries plus ground flax plus a weak granola is 5.4 grams. Same jar, same five minutes, less than half the fiber. For context, NIH's MedlinePlus notes Americans [average about 16 grams of fiber a day](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002470.htm) against a 21 to 38 gram recommendation.

One more line from that FDA page worth keeping: 20 percent of the Daily Value or more per serving counts as high. For fiber that's 5.6 grams. The lazy build misses it. Trade up to the best granola in the next table and the same jar reaches 13.1 grams, which is more than double the bar.

## Why the yogurt layer contributes nothing

USDA lists plain whole-milk Greek yogurt at 0.0 grams of fiber per 100 grams and plain nonfat at 0.0 as well (records 171304 and 170894). Zero. Not "a little." The container can say Greek yogurt in heroic lettering and the fiber line still reads nothing.

What it does bring is 9.0 grams of protein per 100 grams for the whole-milk version and 10.2 for nonfat, which is why [the comparison between eggs and Greek yogurt per dollar](/eggs-vs-greek-yogurt-protein-cost/) gets interesting. Yogurt is the protein layer. Stop asking it to be the fiber layer.

## Your granola decides the total

| Granola | Fiber per 100 g | Label serving | Fiber per label serving | Fiber per 1/2 cup |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bear Naked Original Cinnamon | 9.3 g | 1/2 cup (59 g) | 5.5 g | 5.5 g |
| Quaker Simply Granola, Oats Honey Raisins & Almonds | 9.4 g | 1/2 cup (51 g) | 4.8 g | 4.8 g |
| Nature Valley Oats 'n Honey | 3.7 g | 1/4 cup (27 g) | 1.0 g | 2.0 g |

Manufacturer label data in FoodData Central's Branded Foods dataset, records 2697038, 1459916 and 2746612, checked 2026-07-31. Notice what the serving column is doing. Nature Valley's printed serving is a quarter cup, so its 1-gram number isn't comparable to anything, and two bags that both say "1/2 cup" weigh 59 grams and 51 grams. Read the per-100-gram line, or do the arithmetic for the scoop you actually use.

## How do you keep a yogurt parfait from getting soggy?

Keep the granola separate until you eat. That's the whole rule. Granola sitting against yogurt overnight pulls moisture and gives up its crunch, no matter how nicely you arranged the layers at 10 PM.

Fresh berries can sit right in the yogurt. Frozen fruit goes on top so the juice doesn't water down the jar as it thaws. It may stain the yogurt purple. That's not a failure, that's breakfast admitting it contains berries.

## What should you put in one breakfast parfait?

* **1 cup plain Greek yogurt:** Full-fat or 2%, whichever texture you like. Fiber contribution: zero, and that's fine.
* **1/2 cup raspberries or blackberries:** The two highest-fiber berries in the table above, roughly 4 grams.
* **1/2 cup granola:** Somewhere between 2 and 5.5 grams depending on the bag. This is your variable.
* **1 tablespoon chia seeds or ground flax:** 3.6 grams or 1.9 grams respectively.
* **A small drizzle of maple syrup or honey:** Optional. Taste it first, ripe fruit may be enough.

Spoon in half the yogurt, half the berries, the seeds, then the rest. Granola goes on at the last second. You can make stripes in a clear jar if that brings you joy, but it tastes the same in the chipped cereal bowl.

## How far ahead can you make a yogurt parfait?

The night before is easy, and here's the honest boundary on going further. FoodSafety.gov's [cold food storage chart](https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/cold-food-storage-charts) has no row for yogurt or for an assembled parfait, so there's no federal number to quote you. What is federal: FDA says keep the refrigerator [at 40°F or below](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/refrigerator-thermometers-cold-facts-about-food-safety), and never leave perishable food out more than 2 hours, or 1 hour when the air is above 90°F. Your car in July counts.

So batch a couple of jars, not a week of them, and let the date printed on the yogurt container be the ceiling. FDA's rule here covers produce [bought pre-cut or packaged](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/selecting-and-serving-produce-safely), which it says to keep refrigerated. Fruit you slice yourself for a jar is already going in the fridge, so the same habit applies.

## What can you change when berries aren't available?

Diced pear beats diced apple here: USDA puts raw pear at 3.1 grams of fiber per 100 grams against 2.4 for apple with the skin on (records 169118 and 171688). Keep the peel either way, since that's where a good share of it lives. Sliced banana adds sweetness but softens fast, so that one goes in at breakfast rather than the night before.

The formula doesn't need to become a lifestyle. Creamy base, fruit, one crunchy fiber layer, maybe seeds. Five minutes, one spoon, and no 10 AM excavation of the snack drawer required, though if the drawer wins anyway, [popcorn and almonds aren't equally cheap ways to buy fiber](/popcorn-vs-almonds-fiber-cost/).
