Berries get the poster. Peas get the receipt. I ranked 22 fruits and vegetables by fiber per dollar, fresh and frozen in the same list, because that's the choice in the actual cart: the bag in the freezer door or the pile in the produce section.

Frozen green peas lead at 17.6 g of fiber per dollar ([FDC 170016](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170016/nutrients)). Whole bagged carrots are next at 16.1 g ([FDC 170393](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170393/nutrients)). Fresh blueberries finish at 2.5 g ([FDC 171711](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/171711/nutrients)). Divide 17.6 by 2.5 and you get about 7. The top of the list buys about seven times as much fiber per dollar as the bottom.

A cabbage is 2.5 g of fiber per 100 g and 14.6 g per dollar, because it's $0.78 a pound. You're buying water and fiber together. That's the produce aisle's whole job.

## Frozen isn't automatically the winner

I wanted frozen to sweep. It doesn't.

Peas do. A 32 oz bag at $2.32, 4.5 g of fiber per 100 g, 17.6 g per dollar. Frozen green beans are 10.8 g per dollar from a 32 oz bag at $2.18 ([FDC 169962](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/169962/nutrients)). Frozen broccoli florets are 9.5 g from a 32 oz bag at $2.87 ([FDC 169968](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/169968/nutrients)). Fresh broccoli crowns, the bunch you feel virtuous about, are 6.1 g per dollar at $1.94 a pound ([FDC 170379](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170379/nutrients)). Same vegetable, worse receipt.

Then the fresh rows walk past most of the freezer. Carrots at 16.1 g, cabbage at 14.6 g, sweet potatoes at 13.9 g and $0.98 a pound ([FDC 168482](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168482/nutrients)). Bananas are the fruit that belongs in this conversation: 11.6 g per dollar, $0.65 a pound on the July 2026 BLS average, 2.6 g of fiber per 100 g ([FDC 173944](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173944/nutrients)). A banana isn't winning a fiber contest by weight. It's the fruit that doesn't embarrass itself on price.

Frozen chopped spinach is a nice 8.4 g per dollar from a 12 oz bag at $1.18, and then you're done with the freezer bargains. The rest of the top half is fresh.

If the bag thawed on the counter, the fiber math doesn't matter until you know whether you can still cook it. That's [refreezing frozen vegetables](/can-you-refreeze-frozen-vegetables-after-thawing/), and the short version is the fridge-thaw versus the two-hour counter.

## Why berries finish last

Raspberries have 6.5 g of fiber per 100 g, second only to avocado on this produce list, and they deliver 4.5 g per dollar. A 6 oz box was $2.48. Blackberries are 5.3 g per 100 g and 4.0 g per dollar, a 6 oz box at $2.28. You're paying for a small box of something that tastes like July. The fiber comes along. It isn't why the box is $2.48.

Strawberries are 2.7 g per dollar, a 16 oz box at $3.21 on the July 2026 BLS average, 2.0 g per 100 g ([FDC 167762](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/167762/nutrients)). Blueberries are 2.5 g per dollar, a pint at $2.98, 2.4 g per 100 g. I still buy them. I just don't pretend the pint is the thrifty fiber plan. Pears at 8.0 g and gala apples at 7.3 g are the fruit rows that behave. Oranges are 4.7 g per dollar, closer to the berries than to the bananas.

Avocado is the row that explains the trick. Highest fiber per 100 g in this produce set, at 6.7 g, and seventh on the dollar ranking at 10.4 g, because one fruit was about $0.95. If you want the most fiber in the smallest volume of produce, buy the avocado. If you want the most fiber for the money, buy the peas.

## The edamame row that doesn't have a record

Frozen shelled edamame is on the produce sheet at 8.3 g of fiber per dollar, a 12 oz bag at $2.12, and the USDA id cell is empty. I didn't fill it in.

The fiber sheet's note on that same bag says the row is unresolved. One published fiber figure comes from a cooked edamame record, and the bag you buy is frozen and unprepared. Those aren't interchangeable forms, so the per-100-g cell in the table says unresolved. The 8.3 g stays, because it's the number the produce sheet actually priced. It doesn't get a fake record link.

Everywhere else, the fiber-per-dollar number on this page matches the fiber sheet for the same record. Peas are 17.6 in both. Blueberries are 2.5 in both. If those two files ever disagree, the produce sheet is the ranking and the mismatch gets a label, the way edamame already has one.

## What I'd put in the cart

Peas, carrots, cabbage, sweet potatoes, bananas. That's the top five, and it's a normal week, not a project. The 32 oz bag of peas and the 2 lb bag of carrots are doing more fiber-per-dollar work than the berry clamshell. Frozen green beans are the backup when the peas get boring. Broccoli is fine, and the frozen florets beat the fresh crowns on this score.

Potatoes look cheap at $0.94 a pound and land at 6.3 g of fiber per dollar, because the fiber density is 1.3 g per 100 g with the skin on ([FDC 170027](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170027/nutrients)). Onions are 7.1 g per dollar off 1.7 g per 100 g. Cheap weight isn't the same thing as cheap fiber. The chart is there so a $0.94 potato doesn't get to pretend it's a pea.

Dry lentils lead that dinner. Peas can sit next to them. Price and edible-portion rules for the whole basket are on the [methodology page](/methodology/).
