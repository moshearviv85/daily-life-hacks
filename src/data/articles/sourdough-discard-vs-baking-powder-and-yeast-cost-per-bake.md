The jar looks free because you already bought the flour. I priced what that scoop still holds. A sourdough discard cost per bake, at the audited whole wheat bag, runs from $0.08 to $0.17 for the two amounts bakers actually weigh: 113 g and 227 g, at 100% and at 80% hydration.

113 g is the published half-cup round. 227 g is the cup. The [hydration conversion chart](/sourdough-discard-hydration-conversion-chart/) is the gram split. This page is the dollar on the flour half of that split. The [discard calculator](/tools/sourdough-discard-calculator/) uses the same formula: flour is the discard weight divided by (1 + h), and h is the hydration percent divided by 100. Water is the rest of the scoop. I priced the flour. I didn't price the tap.

## What 113 g and 227 g of discard cost in flour

The bag is the whole wheat flour row in fiber-per-dollar-2026.csv: $3.12 for 5 lb, 2268 g, 77.8 g of fiber per dollar and 96.0 g of protein per dollar ([FDC 168893](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168893/nutrients)). Cost is unrounded flour grams times $3.12, divided by 2268, then the cent. The flour tenths are the display round. A starter you feed with all-purpose isn't this row. The [cheapest whole grains for fiber](/cheapest-whole-grains-for-fiber-per-dollar/) is where that bag sits against the other grains.

| Discard | Hydration | Flour | Water | Flour cost | Record |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 113 g | 100% | 56.5 g | 56.5 g | $0.08 | [FDC 168893](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168893/nutrients) |
| 113 g | 80% | 62.8 g | 50.2 g | $0.09 | [FDC 168893](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168893/nutrients) |
| 227 g | 100% | 113.5 g | 113.5 g | $0.16 | [FDC 168893](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168893/nutrients) |
| 227 g | 80% | 126.1 g | 100.9 g | $0.17 | [FDC 168893](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168893/nutrients) |

At 100%, 113 g really is half and half, 56.5 g each. 227 g is 113.5 g each. At 80%, the unrounded flour in 113 g is 62.777... g, which prints as 62.8 g, and the water prints as 50.2 g. The 227 g row prints 126.1 g of flour and 100.9 g of water. The $0.09 and the $0.17 use the unrounded flour, not the tenth. Round the grams first and then multiply, and you can nudge a cent that the chart doesn't show.

Eighty percent costs more flour because the jar is stiffer. Same discard weight, less water, more of the bag you already opened. That's the whole trick. It isn't a luxury tax on bakers who like a stiff starter.

## Baking powder, yeast, and the buttermilk cup

Unfed discard doesn't replace a yeast packet. It doesn't replace the baking powder in a quick bread either. Those tins aren't in the price files, so I'm not going to invent a $0.39 packet and pretend the study bought it. If the dough was counting on yeast to rise, feed the starter or buy the yeast. The [no-yeast discard recipes](/sourdough-discard-recipes-no-yeast/) still bring baking powder or baking soda when they want height. The jar is the tang and the flour you subtract. The powder is the lift.

Buttermilk is the swap people actually attempt. One cup of lowfat cultured buttermilk is 245 g ([FDC 170874](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170874/nutrients)). This site's cup of discard is 227 g, and at 100% hydration that cup is $0.16 of this flour, not a 245 g dairy pour. The [buttermilk substitute](/sourdough-discard-buttermilk-substitute/) is the gram table: weigh the discard, subtract the flour, add the missing liquid. This page only says what the flour in that scoop was worth. Buttermilk itself isn't priced here, because it isn't in the files.

So the "$0.08 to $0.17" isn't a claim that discard beats the baking aisle by a specific gap. It's the flour you stop weighing out of the bag when the jar goes into the bowl. The yeast and the powder, if the recipe still wants them, are a second receipt this study doesn't have.

## The jar still has a fridge clock

Cheap flour doesn't keep a neglected jar honest. [FoodKeeper](https://www.foodsafety.gov/keep-food-safe/foodkeeper-app) doesn't publish a sourdough-discard row. The [cold-storage chart](https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/cold-food-storage-charts) doesn't either. Keep it at 40°F or below. The planning window on the [how long discard lasts](/how-long-does-sourdough-discard-last/) page is one week from the oldest date on the jar, then bake it or freeze it. That week is this site's plan. It isn't a date USDA printed for discard.

Food held continuously at 0°F can be kept indefinitely for safety on that cold-storage chart, and the month counts there are quality for the foods the chart actually lists. I'm not handing the jar a leftover's three days, and I'm not handing it a freezer month USDA never wrote down. Mold, pink, orange, or a rotten smell ends it whatever the tape says.

Weigh the scoop. Subtract the flour. Pay the $0.08 or the $0.17 in flour you already own, and don't tell yourself the jar replaced the yeast unless the recipe was written that way.
