The desk drawer had a protein bar in it that cost more than the peanuts I forgot to pack. That's the whole annoyance. The cheap protein is at the grocery store. It just has to survive a commute without a refrigerator speech.

Protein rates below come from no-cook-protein-per-dollar-2026.csv, checked against protein-per-dollar-2026.csv. Fiber rates come from high-fiber-snacks-per-dollar-2026.csv, checked against fiber-per-dollar-2026.csv. The filenames stay in the sentence. This page doesn't link the files. What one dollar buys across pantry staples, including the flour you can't pocket, is [what $1 buys in protein versus fiber](/one-dollar-of-protein-vs-one-dollar-of-fiber/).

## Which portable grocery snacks give the most protein and fiber per dollar?

Peanut butter. Ten grams of protein cost $0.20, at 50.7 grams per dollar ([FDC 174266](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/174266/nutrients)). Dry roasted peanuts buy those 10 grams for $0.25, and 5 grams of their fiber cost $0.36 ([FDC 174262](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/174262/nutrients)). Whole carrots are the fiber bargain that isn't a protein food: 5 grams of fiber for $0.31, and the protein file doesn't price them ([FDC 170393](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170393/nutrients)).

Popcorn kernels print 51.3 grams of fiber per dollar in the snack fiber file, and that row has no USDA id. I left it off the ten. An unresolved kernel figure can live in the file. It doesn't get a bar.

Greek yogurt and cottage cheese publish 0.0 grams of fiber per 100 grams on FDC 170894 and FDC 172179. The fiber files leave the cells blank, so there's no honest price for 5 grams of fiber from either tub. The protein rates are real: 27.5 and 26.3 grams per dollar, July 2026 Walmart rows. Carrots, bananas, prunes, and gala apples are in the snack fiber file and not in the protein file. I didn't invent a protein-per-dollar rate for them. The price rules are on the [methodology page](/methodology/).

Almonds lose both races in this set. $0.68 for 10 grams of protein, $0.57 for 5 grams of fiber. Buy them because you want almonds. Don't ask them to be the plan.

## Five snack boxes that clear the good-source lines

[21 CFR 101.54](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-D/section-101.54) sets good source at 10 to 19 percent of the Daily Value per labeled serving. On the [28 gram fiber value and the 50 gram protein value](/fiber-protein-daily-values-explained/), that's 2.8 grams of fiber and 5 grams of protein. High is 20 percent: 5.6 grams of fiber or 10 grams of protein. The [good source of fiber page](/good-source-of-fiber-label-meaning/) is the label version of that rule. These boxes are grocery math. A brand still has to hit the line on its own serving, not on my bag.

Protein in the table is only from foods the protein file prices. Fiber uses the fiber file's grams per 100 grams, times the edible fraction when that fraction is under 1. Cost uses the package price divided by the package grams. Each line is rounded, then added.

| Carrots and sunflower kernels | 100 g carrots, 28 g sunflower kernels | 5.8 g | 5.2 g | $0.35 |
| Yogurt and banana | 170 g Greek yogurt, 185 g banana with the peel | 17.3 g | 3.1 g | $0.89 |
| Cottage cheese and prunes | 100 g cottage cheese, 40 g prunes | 11.1 g | 2.8 g | $0.77 |
| Peanuts and raisins | 28 g dry roasted peanuts, 40 g raisins | 6.8 g | 3.9 g | $0.41 |

The apple box is the one I'd actually pack. The apple is 3.7 grams of fiber and $0.51, from 2.3 grams per 100 grams and a 0.9 edible fraction on [FDC 168204](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168204/nutrients). The 32 gram scoop of peanut butter is 7.1 grams of protein, 1.6 grams of fiber, and $0.14. The apple's own protein isn't in the protein file, so the 7.1 is the peanut butter. 5.3 grams of fiber clears good source and misses high. That's a snack, not a second lunch.

Carrots plus an ounce of sunflower kernels is the cheap one, $0.35. A 100 gram handful of carrots is 2.8 grams of fiber and $0.17. The 28 gram scoop of kernels is 5.8 grams of protein, 2.4 grams of fiber, and $0.18 ([FDC 170562](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170562/nutrients)). Carrot protein stays out of the total. 5.2 grams of fiber is still short of the 5.6 gram high line. Fine. It's a bag of carrots, not a trophy.

Yogurt and a banana need a cold bag, and the yogurt is doing all the protein work. A 170 gram portion is 17.3 grams and $0.63. The banana is 185 grams with the peel, the weight already used in the fiber day file: 3.1 grams of fiber and $0.26, edible fraction 0.64 on [FDC 173944](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173944/nutrients). Yogurt fiber is zero, so I didn't add a fake tenth. 17.3 grams of protein clears the high line. 3.1 grams of fiber just clears good source.

Cottage cheese and prunes are the salty-sweet fridge box. A 100 gram scoop of 4 percent cottage cheese is 11.1 grams of protein and $0.42. Forty grams of prunes, about five, are 2.8 grams of fiber and $0.35 ([FDC 168162](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168162/nutrients)). That's the floor of good source fiber, not a pile of it. Prune protein isn't in the protein file, so it isn't in the 11.1.

Peanuts and raisins are the pocket box, $0.41. An ounce of dry roasted peanuts is 6.8 grams of protein, 2.4 grams of fiber, and $0.17. Forty grams of raisins are 1.5 grams of fiber and $0.24, from 3.7 grams per 100 grams on [FDC 2758980](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/2758980/nutrients). The raisin row in the snack fiber file is 6.3 grams of fiber per dollar. Raisin protein isn't in the protein file. The 6.8 grams are the peanuts. Together the fiber is 3.9 grams, which is a good source and not a high one.

## What has to stay cold

Peanut butter, peanuts, sunflower kernels, prunes, raisins, and the apple can live in a bag for the morning. Carrots want the same bag, not a hot car seat in July. Yogurt and cottage cheese want a cold pack, or you eat them before you leave. This isn't a canned-fish list for a power outage. It's the stuff that fits next to a laptop.

Almonds at $0.68 for 10 grams of protein are how the drawer gets expensive while looking responsible. The peanut butter scoop does the protein job for $0.20. The carrot handful does the fiber job for $0.31. Flour and dry lentils still beat every snack on the [dollar comparison](/one-dollar-of-protein-vs-one-dollar-of-fiber/), and they don't fit in a coat pocket. That's the trade. Cheap grams, or grams you can carry.
