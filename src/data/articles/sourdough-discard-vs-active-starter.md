Sourdough discard vs starter confuses people because it's one jar playing two parts. You remove some, you feed the rest, and both are still flour, water, and the same culture. The recipe cares which moment you scooped.

The [easy sourdough discard recipes](/easy-sourdough-discard-recipes-beginners/) want the unfed scoop. A loaf that has to rise on wild yeast wants the fed one, after it has actually risen. I'd rather read that off the card than find out at dinner.

## Same jar, different moment in the feeding cycle

[King Arthur](https://www.kingarthurbaking.com/blog/2024/04/18/sourdough-discard) defines discard as the portion you take out during routine maintenance, so the feeding has room and the culture stays healthy. You can throw that portion away, or you can bake with it. They use "unfed" for that same portion in bread recipes. They use "ripe" for starter that's ready to bake bread.

Ripe, on their [create guide](https://www.kingarthurbaking.com/learn/guides/sourdough/create), means reliably very bubbly and doubled within 6 to 8 hours of feeding. This site's same-day pizza page says "active" for that bubbly, recently working jar. Different word, same moment. If your starter isn't there yet because it's new, [discard from a new starter](/can-you-use-sourdough-discard-from-a-new-starter/) isn't either of these columns.

## Sourdough discard vs starter

| | Discard, unfed | Active, ripe starter |
|---|---|---|
| When it was fed | Removed before the feeding. King Arthur. | Fed, then doubled and very bubbly within 6 to 8 hours. King Arthur. |
| Acidity and body | More acidic once it sits unfed, and thinner after it collapses. Pantry Mama and sourdoughstarter.com. | Sharply sour but clean when it's healthy. King Arthur. A neglected jar isn't this column. |
| Lift | Adds flavor, doesn't do much leavening. King Arthur. Usually not enough to fully leaven bread alone. Nordic Ware. | What King Arthur uses to raise bread without manufactured yeast. |
| Best fit on this site | Pancakes with baking soda, crackers, baking-powder pizza crust. | A recipe that asks the starter to raise the dough. The same-day pizza here adds instant yeast instead. |

## Recipes where discard is the right call

Discard earns its spot when something else lifts the batter.

The pancakes in the beginner recipes use **baking soda**. King Arthur's point is that the discard brings tang, and the soda brings the puff when it hits that acid. You don't wait for the jar to double.

The [thin pizza crust](/easy-sourdough-discard-pizza-dough-no-yeast/) uses **227 grams** of discard and **baking powder**, with about 40 minutes including the preheat. No yeast rise. Expect a thin crust, not a puffy rim. The no-roll crackers are the oven's job. They're spread thin and baked until the center is firm. Nothing in that card needs a ripe starter.

Those are also the weights on the [amount planner](/what-to-make-with-sourdough-discard-by-amount/). Match the grams first. Then check that the recipe wanted discard, not a starter at peak.

## Recipes that need an active starter

Naturally leavened bread is the other list. King Arthur's ready starter is the one that doubles on that 6 to 8 hour clock, and they built it so it can raise dough without commercial yeast. A flat, unfed jar isn't that tool. Nordic Ware says discard usually doesn't have enough strength to fully leaven bread on its own.

Two attributed workarounds exist, and they aren't the same move.

King Arthur says you can make some breads with unfed starter, and that it's best if the starter was fed at least once in the past week. A neglected starter won't make great bread. That's their caveat, on the discard article, not a promise that any loaf recipe will accept a month-old jar.

The other workaround is commercial yeast. Nordic Ware says discard can be used in bread recipes that include additional yeast. This site's [same-day sourdough pizza dough](/how-to-make-sourdough-pizza-dough-same-day/) is that hybrid. Cold discard for flavor, instant yeast for the rise, in about three to four hours. The page also says an active, bubbly starter can give a lighter lift, and that you don't have to feed first if the yeast is in the bowl.

## Can you turn discard back into a starter?

Yes, when the discard came from an established starter and the jar isn't spoiled. King Arthur says feed discard with flour and water and you can grow a starter from it, including a jar you hand to someone else. Pantry Mama's amounts are **50 grams of discard, 50 grams of flour, and 50 grams of water**, repeated until it bubbles and doubles.

That restart isn't instant ripe starter for tonight's loaf. You feed it until it behaves like the right-hand column. And it isn't a rescue for the first removals from a brand-new culture. Those still get thrown out until the rise test is real. Mold, pink or orange, or a rotten smell isn't a feeding project. King Arthur says throw that out.

## How to read a recipe that says unfed or ripe

"Unfed" and "discard" are King Arthur's words for the portion you removed and didn't feed. "Ripe" is their word for fed starter at the doubling point. "Active" on this site's pizza page means that bubbly jar.

If a discard recipe's author says you may swap in active starter, believe them for that card only. That Sourdough Gal says her 1,000 gram waffles can use active starter. Kneaded That says the same for the 100 gram lemon muffins, and says to weigh it, because a bubbly starter takes up more room in a cup than discard does. Our pancake card wasn't written that way. Ripe starter is less sour and fuller of gas, so the batter won't match the discard version exactly. The baking soda is still the lift King Arthur is talking about.

Read the leavening line before you read the romance. Soda, powder, or eggs mean discard is welcome. "Ripe starter" and no commercial yeast means feed the jar and wait for the double.
