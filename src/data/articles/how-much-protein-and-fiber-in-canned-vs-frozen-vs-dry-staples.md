The freezer door, the can shelf, and the dry-bean bag are three answers to the same Tuesday. I've bought all three and told myself they were the same food. They aren't. The dry bag wins the dollar. The can wins the clock. The freezer wins only if you have the space and you read the sodium.

A comparison serving here is 20 grams of protein. Cost is 20 divided by the grams-per-dollar rate, rounded to the cent. Protein rates are protein-per-dollar-2026.csv. Fiber rates are fiber-per-dollar-2026.csv. Sodium is milligrams per 100 g from sodium-per-dollar-2026.csv, except two cans that aren't in that sheet. The filenames stay in the sentence. This page doesn't link the files.

## How much protein and fiber do canned, frozen, and dry staples return?

Dry green split peas return 73.9 g of protein and 71.0 g of fiber per dollar ([FDC 172428](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/172428/nutrients)). Frozen green peas return 20.4 g of protein and 17.6 g of fiber ([FDC 170016](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170016/nutrients)). That's about 3.6 times less protein per dollar once the peas have been frozen. Canned black beans return 30.1 g of protein and 34.4 g of fiber ([FDC 175188](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/175188/nutrients)), about 1.9 times less protein than the dry black beans at 58.0 g and 41.6 g ([FDC 173734](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173734/nutrients)).

Twenty grams of protein costs $0.27 from the split peas, $0.34 from the dry black beans, $0.66 from the black bean can, and $0.98 from the frozen peas. The $0.88 can looks cheaper than the $3.38 bag. It's cheaper as a thing you pick up. It isn't cheaper as protein. Dry black beans at 41.6 g of fiber per dollar against the can at 34.4 g is only about 1.2 times. The protein gap and the sodium gap are the ones that move the receipt. The bean-only markup is the [canned versus dry beans table](/canned-vs-dry-beans-cost-and-sodium-per-serving/). The dry bags alone are [beans that do both jobs per dollar](/beans-double-win-fiber-protein/).

## Sodium is the column the sale tag skips

Five milligrams versus 384. That's the black bean pair, per 100 g, and 384 divided by 5 is 76.8, about 77 times. Frozen green peas at 108 mg against dry split peas at 5 mg are 21.6 times, about 22. Canned kidney beans are 296 mg ([FDC 173741](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173741/nutrients)). Canned chickpeas are 278 mg on the regular record. Tuna is 247 mg and pink salmon is 403 mg ([FDC 173709](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173709/nutrients), [FDC 173690](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173690/nutrients)). The dry rows here are 5 mg. The freezer has one quiet bag and one that isn't.

[FDA 21 CFR 101.61](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/section-101.61) is the sodium-claim rule. A low-sodium claim is 140 mg or less per reference amount when that amount is over 30 g. The rule judges the reference amount, not a flat 100 g, so this column isn't a verdict on the label. It's the only unit the three files share. Every can on this page is already past 140 mg per 100 g. Frozen peas at 108 mg aren't, and the dry bags aren't close.

The 384 mg and 278 mg figures aren't in the sodium file. They're the regular-sodium numbers in the protein-sheet note for those two records: 384 versus 138 for the low-sodium black bean record, and 278 versus 132 for the low-sodium chickpea record. The priced cans aren't labeled low sodium, so the regular record is the one in the table. The same split is written up on the [canned versus dried beans nutrition page](/canned-beans-vs-dried-beans-nutrition/).

One price trap, then I'll drop it. Dry black beans are 58.0 g of protein per dollar on a 2 lb bag at $3.38 in the protein sheet ([FDC 173734](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173734/nutrients)). The sodium sheet uses that same record and a different receipt, $2.42 for a 2 lb bag. I used the protein sheet for the rate and the sodium sheet only for the 5 mg. Mixing those stickers would invent a third bag. Split peas, frozen peas, and edamame use the same package price on both sheets.

Frozen shelled edamame is the hatched bar ([FDC 168410](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168410/nutrients)). Protein and sodium are the unprepared bag: 18.0 g per dollar and 6 mg. The fiber sheet prints 8.3 g per dollar, marks the row unresolved, and leaves the FDC id blank. Its note says that fiber density came from a cooked record, not this bag. The hatch means published, not settled. Shop the 18.0 g and the 6 mg.

## When the can or the freezer is worth the markup

I buy the can on the nights the soak didn't happen. That's a $0.32 premium on 20 grams of black-bean protein, $0.66 instead of $0.34, plus a sodium column that doesn't care about your schedule. Frozen peas are the expensive middle: $0.98 for the same 20 grams, 108 mg of sodium, and a drawer you might not have. Edamame keeps the sodium at 6 mg and returns 18.0 g of protein per dollar, about 4.1 times less than the split peas. You're paying for a freezer bag that behaves, not for a deal. [Cook the dried beans](/how-to-cook-dried-beans-from-scratch/) when the evening is free. If you only needed 10 grams of fiber on a plate you were already making, the cheaper add-ins are on the [10 gram fiber list](/cheapest-ways-to-add-10-grams-of-fiber-to-any-meal/).

## Tuna and salmon aren't a bean substitute

Chunk light tuna returns 22.4 g of protein per dollar ([FDC 173709](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173709/nutrients)). Pink salmon returns 21.6 g ([FDC 173690](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173690/nutrients)). Those rates sit next to canned chickpeas at 22.0 g, which is a trick of the chart. The fish has no fiber row, and the salmon's 403 mg of sodium is the widest bar on the page. You buy them for protein with no stove, not for the split-pea rate.

The rest of the canned fish receipt, sardines included, is [canned fish protein per dollar](/cheapest-canned-fish-and-seafood-protein-per-dollar/). If the cupboard is the whole plan, dry bags first, cans when the clock won, freezer only for the bag whose sodium you already checked.
