Blanch, chill fast, dry well, freeze flat. That's the whole method, and the step people skip is the first one. The [National Center for Home Food Preservation](https://nchfp.uga.edu/how/freeze/general-freezing-information/blanching-vegetables/) calls blanching "a must for almost all vegetables to be frozen," because it stops the enzyme actions that cause loss of flavor, color and texture.

Raw vegetables in a bag will freeze. They just come out looking like they regret it.

## Why can't you skip the blanching?

Because freezing slows enzymes without stopping them. USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service explains it in its [freezing guidance](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/freezing-and-food-safety): enzymes in vegetables promote chemical reactions before and after harvest, freezing only slows that activity, and most vegetables that freeze well are low acid and require brief, partial cooking to prevent deterioration.

[University of Minnesota Extension](https://extension.umn.edu/preserving-and-preparing/how-blanch-vegetables-safe-preservation) adds the other half: blanching also kills bacteria that might be on the vegetables, since many bacteria are destroyed at boiling water or steam temperatures. It lists the short exception list, the vegetables that don't need blanching at all: diced or cut onions, green onions, leeks, and peppers.

NCHFP is blunt about doing it halfway. "Underblanching stimulates the activity of enzymes and is worse than no blanching."

## How long do you blanch each vegetable?

Follow the table, not your instincts. These are NCHFP's water blanching times unless noted.

| Vegetable | Blanching time |
|---|---|
| Beans, snap, green or wax | 3 minutes |
| Green peas | 1.5 minutes |
| Carrots, diced or sliced | 2 minutes |
| Carrots, small whole | 5 minutes |
| Broccoli florets | 3 minutes in water, 5 in steam |
| Cauliflower florets | 3 minutes |
| Corn, whole kernel | 4 minutes |
| Greens, other than collards | 2 minutes |
| Brussels sprouts, medium | 4 minutes |
| Summer squash | 3 minutes |

*Source: [NCHFP Blanching Vegetables](https://nchfp.uga.edu/how/freeze/general-freezing-information/blanching-vegetables/), extracted from So Easy to Preserve, 6th ed. Times are for water blanching except where steam is noted.*

Use one gallon of water per pound of vegetables. NCHFP says the water should return to a boil within a minute, and if it doesn't, you're doing too much at once. Start the timer when the boil comes back.

Do not use the microwave. Both NCHFP and [NDSU Extension](https://www.ndsu.edu/agriculture/extension/publications/freezing-vegetables) say research shows some enzymes may not be inactivated by microwave blanching, and NCHFP adds it won't save time or energy anyway.

## What happens right after the boil?

The cooling, and it matters as much as the heat. NCHFP says to plunge the basket straight into a large quantity of cold water at 60F or below, using about a pound of ice per pound of vegetables, and that cooling should take the same amount of time as the blanching did.

Then drain them properly. NCHFP is direct that extra moisture causes a loss of quality when vegetables are frozen. I lay mine on a towel for a few minutes, which feels fussy and is the difference between loose pieces and one solid brick.

## Which vegetables are not worth freezing?

NDSU names them: cabbage, green onions, lettuce and other salad greens, radishes, cucumbers, and tomatoes except as juice or for cooking. It also flags that uncooked potatoes often develop an uncharacteristic sweet flavor when frozen.

The pattern is the same one that runs through the whole freezer aisle. Anything whose job is crunch does not survive an ice crystal. That's also why [fresh stir fry vegetables](/high-fiber-stir-fry-vegetables/) stay a fresh-produce purchase, and why [frozen vs fresh produce](/frozen-vs-fresh-produce-when-to-buy/) is a split decision rather than a winner.

## How should you package and freeze them?

NDSU says containers must be moisture-vapor resistant, durable and leak-proof, and specifically rules out plastic sandwich bags, bread wrappers, and cottage cheese or sour cream cartons for long-term freezer storage. NCHFP adds that loss of moisture is what causes freezer burn.

Speed matters too. FSIS says to freeze food as fast as possible, never stack packages that are still freezing, and spread them in one layer on shelves, stacking only once they're solid. It notes a food two inches thick should freeze completely in about two hours.

* **Label everything.** NCHFP says to include the name, added ingredients, packaging date, and amount. A marker on the bag beats a guess in February.
* **Freeze flat.** Faster freeze, easier stacking, and you can snap off a portion.
* **Leave headspace** in rigid containers so the food can expand without breaking the seal.
* **Store at 0F or below.** NCHFP puts fruits and vegetables at roughly 8 to 12 months there.

When it comes back out, [cook it from frozen](/how-to-cook-frozen-vegetables-without-the-mush/) rather than thawing it first.
