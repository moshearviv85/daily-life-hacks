The dairy aisle has gotten aggressive. It used to just be milk and a few tubs of butter. Now, there's an entire wall of high protein options staring you down, demanding you make a choice. 

When it comes to picking a powerhouse protein base, the cottage cheese vs Greek yogurt debate is the one that trips people up the most. They look similar, they sit next to each other on the shelf, and they both boast massive protein numbers. So which one should you actually buy? The short answer is both, because they do different jobs in your kitchen.

Which has more protein? Per 100 grams, 4% cottage cheese does: 11.12 grams, against 10.19 grams for plain nonfat Greek yogurt.

When the tubs come in different sizes and both are wearing sale stickers, the [grocery unit price calculator](/tools/grocery-unit-price-calculator/) settles the cheaper-per-ounce question before aisle math starts lying to you.

## Which has more protein, cottage cheese or Greek yogurt?

Cottage cheese, but barely. A half cup of cottage cheese carries about 12 to 14 grams of protein against 10 to 12 grams for plain nonfat Greek yogurt. Per dollar the order flips: Greek yogurt delivers 27.5 grams of [protein per dollar](/protein-per-dollar-cheapest-protein-sources/) and 4% cottage cheese 26.3, using USDA data and July 2026 prices. The real difference is sodium, not protein.

| | Protein per 1/2 cup | Protein per $1 |
|---|---|---|
| Cottage cheese (4%) | 12 to 14 g | 26.3 g |
| Greek yogurt (plain, nonfat) | 10 to 12 g | 27.5 g |

Same foods, weighed. USDA SR Legacy lists 11.12 grams of protein per 100 grams for creamed cottage cheese ([FDC 172179](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/172179/nutrients)) and 10.19 grams per 100 grams for plain nonfat Greek yogurt ([FDC 170894](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170894/nutrients)). Cottage cheese still has more at an equal weight. A cup isn't one number, because USDA publishes two cottage-cheese cups and no cup at all for this yogurt.

| | Protein per 100 g | Portion USDA publishes | Protein in that portion |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Cottage cheese, 4%, small curd, not packed | 11.12 g | 1 cup, 225 g | 25.0 g |
| Cottage cheese, 4%, large curd, not packed | 11.12 g | 1 cup, 210 g | 23.4 g |
| Greek yogurt, plain, nonfat | 10.19 g | 1 container, 170 g | 17.3 g |
| Same yogurt, scaled to the 225 g small-curd cup | 10.19 g | 225 g, not a cup on this record | 22.9 g |

Those portion grams are the per-100-gram number times the USDA weight, then rounded to one decimal. 11.12 across 225 grams is 25.02, shown as 25.0. Across 210 grams it's 23.35, shown as 23.4. The yogurt container is 10.19 across 170 grams, 17.32, shown as 17.3. The same 10.19 across 225 grams is 22.93, shown as 22.9, still under the small-curd cottage cheese cup. Half of that 225 gram cup is 112.5 grams, about 12.5 grams of protein, inside the 12 to 14 gram half-cup range above. The yogurt record simply doesn't call 225 grams a cup.

## How does cottage cheese vs Greek yogurt protein compare by fat level?

The half-cup answer above stays put. Fat level is what moves the per-100-gram and per-cup numbers ranking pages actually publish.

These six rows are plain SR Legacy records: nonfat, 2%, and 4% cottage cheese, then plain nonfat, lowfat, and whole-milk Greek yogurt. The lowfat Greek yogurt row is the 2% tub. It lists 1.92 grams of fat per 100 grams, and USDA filed it as "lowfat" instead of printing 2% on the name.

Calories, sodium, and calcium are per 100 grams, so a heavier cup can't pad those columns. Cottage cheese protein per cup uses the cup on that same record. Nonfat cottage cheese here is uncreamed dry curd, and its cup weighs 145 grams, not the 210 to 226 gram creamed cup. Plain Greek yogurt records still don't print a cup. That column scales the row's own protein to 245 grams, the 1 cup weight on the plain nonfat ([FDC 2705424](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/2705424/nutrients)), plain lowfat ([FDC 2705423](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/2705423/nutrients)), and plain whole-milk ([FDC 2705422](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/2705422/nutrients)) survey records.

The linked food pages round some protein figures to three significant figures. This table keeps the SR Legacy amounts, same as the 11.12 and 10.19 above. Cup protein is that amount times the gram weight, divided by 100, rounded to one decimal. Protein per 100 kcal is protein divided by calories, times 100, same rounding. 10.34 across 145 grams is 14.993, shown as 15.0. 10.45 across 226 grams is 23.617, shown as 23.6. The 4% cups stay 25.0 and 23.4. Across 245 grams, 10.19, 9.95, and 9.00 come out to 24.966, 24.378, and 22.05, shown as 25.0, 24.4, and 22.1.

Cottage cheese sits at 308 to 372 mg of sodium per 100 grams, and plain Greek yogurt sits at 34 to 36 mg, a gap of 272 to 338 mg, or 8.6 to 10.9 times the sodium at the same weight. Calcium is tighter, and it doesn't land the same way on every row: 2% cottage cheese holds 111 mg and stands with the Greek yogurts at 100 to 115 mg, while 4% and dry nonfat cottage cheese trail at 83 and 86 mg. Per calorie, plain nonfat Greek yogurt wins at 17.3 grams of protein per 100 kcal, and whole-milk Greek yogurt finishes last at 9.3.

Let's look at the numbers. They're surprisingly similar. 

A standard half cup serving of plain, nonfat Greek yogurt usually gives you around ten to twelve grams of protein. A standard half cup of cottage cheese gives you about twelve to fourteen grams. They're both absolute heavyweights in the protein department.

The main nutritional difference is the sodium. Cottage cheese is salty. It has a significantly higher sodium content than Greek yogurt, which is naturally lower in sodium and much more tart. Greek yogurt also contains active cultures, which may support gut health, while only some brands of cottage cheese include live cultures. 

## How do cottage cheese and Greek yogurt taste different?

This is where people draw battle lines. 

Greek yogurt is smooth, thick, and tangy. It acts like sour cream. It blends beautifully into sweet things and provides a sharp, bright contrast to fruit and honey. 

Cottage cheese is lumpy. There's no getting around the curds. It's savory, salty, and mild. If you hate the texture of cottage cheese, you're not alone. But writing it off completely is a mistake, because it's got a secret weapon: it blends perfectly. If you run cottage cheese through a food processor or a blender, it turns into a silky, savory cream that behaves entirely differently than yogurt.

## When should you use Greek yogurt?

Because of its tangy flavor profile, Greek yogurt is your best friend for anything sweet or bright. 

Use it as the base for a parfait with berries and granola. Mix it into your overnight oats for creaminess. Those oats are the fiber in that bowl: old-fashioned rolled oats return 35.8 grams of fiber per dollar in the [fiber per dollar ranking](/fiber-per-dollar-cheapest-high-fiber-foods/). Use it to tone down a spicy taco by dolloping it on top instead of sour cream. It also bakes incredibly well, keeping muffins and cakes moist without adding heavy oil. If a recipe needs a bright, acidic lift, grab the yogurt.

## When should you use cottage cheese?

Cottage cheese shines when things get savory. 

It's the perfect base for a lunch bowl. Top it with halved cherry tomatoes, everything bagel seasoning, and a drizzle of olive oil. It's salty and satisfying. 

If you blend it, cottage cheese becomes a magical ingredient. You can stir blended cottage cheese into marinara sauce to make a high protein, creamy pasta sauce. You can blend it into pancake batter to add massive amounts of protein without changing the taste. Because it lacks the tartness of yogurt, it hides much better in savory dishes and cooked meals.

## Can you swap one for the other in cooking?

If you love experimenting in the kitchen, both of these are great for stealthy upgrades. 

Greek yogurt can replace oil or buttermilk in many baking recipes. It keeps banana bread soft and helps pancakes rise. Just remember that heating yogurt too fast on the stove can cause it to separate and look grainy, so temper it slowly if you're adding it to a hot soup.

Cottage cheese, especially blended, is a fantastic substitute for ricotta. Use it in lasagna or stuffed shells. Because it's already salty, you might want to hold back on adding extra salt to the dish until you taste it. It melts down beautifully into a creamy layer that nobody will ever guess is packed with protein.

## Reading the labels

When you buy either of these, the label matters. 

Avoid the ones with fruit on the bottom. They're usually hiding a staggering amount of added sugar. You're much better off buying plain yogurt or plain cottage cheese and adding your own honey, fruit, or jam at home. This way, you control the sweetness, and cinnamon, vanilla, citrus zest, and cocoa are [ways to add flavor without reaching for more sugar](/add-flavor-without-more-sugar-tricks/) at all. 

Also, don't fear the fat. Fat free versions are fine, but two percent or whole milk versions taste significantly better and often keep you full longer. The fat helps carry the flavor and improves the texture dramatically.

Deciding between cottage cheese vs Greek yogurt isn't a competition. They're just two different tools. Keep yogurt for your sweet mornings and cottage cheese for your savory afternoons, and your fridge will be a much happier place.
