The honest caveat: peas are the star of the freezer, and a $1.58 bag of carrots is right behind them.

## How much more fiber does frozen actually buy?

Peas against broccoli is a slightly unfair fight, so compare the same vegetable both ways. Frozen broccoli florets: 9.5 grams of fiber per dollar. Fresh broccoli crowns: 6.1. About 1.6 times more fiber, identical vegetable, one aisle apart.

The mechanism is boring. That pound of fresh crowns ran $1.94, or roughly 43 cents per 100 grams. The 32 ounce bag of frozen florets ran $2.87, which is about 32 cents per 100 grams, and the frozen florets also test a little denser at 3.0 grams of fiber per 100 grams against 2.6 for the fresh crowns. Cheaper per gram, more fiber per gram. Nothing mysterious.

Peas take it further. At 4.5 grams of fiber per 100 grams and 26 cents per 100 grams, frozen green peas hit 17.6 grams per dollar, the best number any vegetable posted in our [fiber per dollar study](/fiber-per-dollar-cheapest-high-fiber-foods/). You can dump a couple of handfuls straight from the bag into a [20 minute bean and rice skillet](/quick-20-minute-high-fiber-meals-for-busy-days/). No thawing, no chopping, no guilt about the crisper drawer.

The tidy "frozen beats fresh" headline falls apart in row two. Bagged whole carrots at 16.1 grams per dollar beat every frozen bag except peas. A 78 cent head of green cabbage at 14.6 beats frozen green beans, frozen broccoli, and frozen spinach. Meanwhile the bag of pre-chopped kale, which reads like the virtuous choice, manages 7.5.

So the real split isn't frozen versus fresh. It's cheap staples versus everything sold in a convenient small package.

## When is fresh produce still the right buy?

Whenever texture is the entire point, and whenever you're buying the cheap end of the fresh aisle.

Nothing in the freezer makes a slaw. Nothing in the freezer roasts with browned edges. Frozen vegetables come out of the bag already blanched and full of ice crystals, so they go soft, which is fine in soup and disappointing on a sheet pan. That $1.94 pound of fresh crowns earns its price the moment you want actual crunch in a [vegetable stir fry](/high-fiber-stir-fry-vegetables/).

And the cheap fresh staples are genuinely good value. Green cabbage at 14.6 grams of fiber per dollar is one of the better deals in the store, which is why a pot of [cabbage soup](/fiber-rich-soup-for-weight-loss-cabbage/) costs almost nothing to make. Carrots keep for weeks in the fridge. Sweet potatoes at 13.9 sit on the counter and ask for nothing.

## What actually belongs in the cart?

Buy the frozen version of anything you plan to cook into something. Peas, green beans, spinach, broccoli for soup. Buy the fresh version of anything you plan to eat raw, roast hard, or slice thin. Carrots, cabbage, whatever you're actually going to finish.

Then be honest about the third category: the fresh vegetables you buy because they look responsible and then throw away nine days later. Those score zero grams of fiber per dollar, and no ranking table can save them. If that's a familiar pattern, the freezer is doing you a favor by being patient.
