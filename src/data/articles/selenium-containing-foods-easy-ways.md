Selenium sounds like something that belongs on a lab label, not a grocery list. I get it. The name doesn't exactly scream Tuesday dinner.

But selenium foods aren't strange. They're eggs, canned tuna, sardines, chicken, turkey, pork, pasta, mushrooms, dairy, and Brazil nuts. Normal groceries. The kind you buy while half-thinking about whether you still have paper towels at home.

What makes selenium different is that it's one of the few nutrients where you can overshoot using nothing but food, and the food that does it sits in the bulk bin looking innocent. So this covers both directions: how to hit the number, and where the ceiling is.

If you're planning food variety instead of chasing one nutrient, this guide to [zinc-containing foods for weekly meals](/zinc-containing-foods-weekly-meals/) uses the same normal-groceries approach.

## How much selenium do you need a day?

Adults 19 and older need 55 micrograms a day, men and women alike, according to the [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Selenium-HealthProfessional/). Pregnancy raises it to 60 mcg and breastfeeding to 70 mcg.

The ceiling matters just as much, and most food articles bury it. The tolerable upper intake level for adults is 400 mcg a day, and the [Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/selenium/) applies that same 400 mcg figure to pregnant and lactating women too. It's a chronic daily ceiling, not a one-day poison line. NIH describes selenosis as the result of chronically high intakes, showing up as hair loss, brittle or lost nails, a garlic odor on the breath, and a metallic taste in the mouth. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002414.htm) adds that selenium toxicity is rare in the United States.

Both numbers are tiny, which is the whole point. NIH notes that selenium intakes in North America, even in low-selenium regions, run well above the RDA.

## Which foods have the most selenium?

Here's the actual ranking, pulled from the NIH food table. Percent DV runs against the 55 mcg Daily Value the FDA uses for adults.

|---|---|---|---|
| Brazil nuts | 1 oz (6 to 8 nuts) | 544 mcg | 989% |
| Tuna, yellowfin, cooked | 3 oz | 92 mcg | 167% |
| Sardines, canned in oil | 3 oz | 45 mcg | 82% |
| Shrimp, cooked | 3 oz | 42 mcg | 76% |
| Pork chop, broiled | 3 oz | 37 mcg | 67% |
| Beef steak, bottom round | 3 oz | 37 mcg | 67% |
| Spaghetti, cooked | 1 cup | 33 mcg | 60% |
| Turkey, roasted | 3 oz | 26 mcg | 47% |
| Cod, Pacific, cooked | 3 oz | 24 mcg | 44% |
| Chicken, light meat, roasted | 3 oz | 22 mcg | 40% |
| Cottage cheese, 1% | 1 cup | 20 mcg | 36% |
| Egg, hard-boiled | 1 large | 15 mcg | 27% |
| Oatmeal, cooked with water | 1 cup | 13 mcg | 24% |
| Brown rice, cooked | 1 cup | 12 mcg | 22% |
| Yogurt, plain, low fat | 1 cup | 8 mcg | 15% |
| Lentils, boiled | 1 cup | 6 mcg | 11% |

Source: NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, selenium fact sheet for health professionals.

Look at where the legumes land. A whole cup of lentils is 6 mcg, about a ninth of the day. Lentils are excellent food for other reasons. They're just not your selenium plan.

## How many Brazil nuts is too many?

Six. That's the honest answer, and almost nobody prints it.

NIH puts one Brazil nut at 68 to 91 micrograms and says flatly that Brazil nuts "could cause selenium toxicity if consumed regularly." Run that against the 400 mcg ceiling: five nuts a day lands somewhere between 340 and 455 mcg depending on the nut, and six nuts clears 400 even at the low end of the range. The standard 1-ounce serving, the 6 to 8 nuts most labels assume, averages 544 mcg.

One nut covers the entire adult RDA by itself. Two is already double. Chop one over oatmeal or yogurt and move on. A fistful every afternoon while you answer email is the version that walks you toward the ceiling.

One caveat NIH flags directly: that 544 mcg average comes from USDA FoodData Central, and values from other analyses vary widely. You don't actually know what's in your bag, which is one more argument for one nut instead of five.

If you hate Brazil nuts, look at the table again. You're fine. They're convenient, not mandatory.

## Seafood and eggs do the everyday work

Three ounces of cooked yellowfin tuna delivers 92 mcg, about a day and two thirds. Sardines bring 45, shrimp 42, Pacific cod 24. NIH explains why protein foods dominate the list: selenium is bound to protein in food, so high-protein foods tend to be the best sources.

Canned tuna is the practical pick. Cheap, shelf-stable, and lunch in about four minutes if you've got bread, crackers, or a sad bag of greens that needs a purpose.

Eggs are the breakfast shortcut. One large hard-boiled egg is 15 mcg, so two eggs plus a slice of whole wheat bread (8 mcg) puts you around 38 mcg, roughly 70 percent of the day before 8 AM. Eggs pull the same weight on the grocery bill, which is what [the eggs against Greek yogurt comparison on protein per dollar](/eggs-vs-greek-yogurt-protein-cost/) is really measuring.

## Do meat, dairy, and grains count?

More than most lists admit. NIH says the major food sources of selenium in American diets are breads, grains, meat, poultry, seafood, and eggs. Not Brazil nuts. Bread and chicken.

A cup of cooked spaghetti carries 33 mcg, which beats an egg and beats 3 ounces of roasted chicken. Turkey brings 26 mcg per 3 ounces, a broiled pork chop 37, a bottom round steak 37. Thighs are usually the better buy at the counter anyway, and [the price gap between thighs and breast per gram of protein](/chicken-thighs-vs-breast-protein-cost/) is wider than most people assume.

Dairy pitches in quietly: a cup of 1% cottage cheese is 20 mcg, plain low fat yogurt 8, 1% milk 6.

Beans and greens are the soft spot. A cup of canned baked beans is 13 mcg, lentils 6, half a cup of frozen spinach 5. Grilled portabella mushrooms do better at 13 mcg per half cup. Eat all of them, just not as your selenium strategy.

## Does soil really change what's in your food?

For plants, yes. NIH says selenium in plant foods depends on the amount and form of selenium in the soil, plus soil pH and organic matter, so concentrations vary widely by geography. A cup of oatmeal grown in one place isn't the same as a cup grown somewhere else.

For animal foods, mostly no, and this is the part almost every roundup skips. NIH says soil selenium doesn't substantially affect animal products, because animals hold predictable tissue concentrations through their own homeostatic mechanisms and formulated livestock feeds carry selenium at consistent levels. So the tuna, chicken, and egg numbers in that table travel better than the oatmeal number does.

## Easy selenium meal ideas for the week

* **Breakfast:** Two eggs with whole grain toast, about 38 mcg. Or oatmeal with one chopped Brazil nut, which blows past the day on its own.
* **Lunch:** Canned tuna sandwich, sardines on crackers, or a turkey wrap.
* **Dinner:** Chicken thighs or a pork chop with brown rice.
* **Pantry night:** Spaghetti with marinara and a fried egg on top, roughly 48 mcg from the pasta and egg alone.
* **Snack:** Cottage cheese, yogurt, or shrimp cocktail if the store had a deal.

Rotate two or three of those and you clear 55 mcg without tracking a thing. Some weeks it's pasta twice because groceries got expensive and life got loud, and that still works out.

The number actually worth remembering is the other one. 400 mcg is the ceiling, one Brazil nut is 68 to 91, and it's the only thing in an American grocery store that can walk you up to that line by accident.
