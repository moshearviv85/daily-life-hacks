That row is unresolved rather than an exact match: the USDA nutrition value describes air-popped popcorn, so we converted it to the unpopped-kernel basis that matches the $2.28 bag we priced, which pulled the figure down from the 57.7 we first published. USDA publishes nothing for unpopped kernels, so the 51.3 is our calculation. Among rows with an exact USDA match, whole carrots lead at 16.1 grams per dollar.

## Is popcorn the cheapest high-fiber snack?

By this dataset, yes, with a caveat about where the number comes from. A 2 lb bag of plain kernels was priced at $2.28 and works out to 51.3 grams of fiber per dollar, more than triple the next snack on the list. The nutrition match is USDA's air-popped record, so we converted it to a kernel basis rather than pretending the two forms are the same thing; that is why the row is labelled unresolved and why the figure fell from 57.7. Among the seven exact USDA matches in this snack subset, carrots lead at 16.1 grams per dollar.

The catch is the word "plain." This ranking is for kernels you pop yourself, on the stove or in a paper bag in the microwave, not the pre-bagged butter-blasted stuff. Popped at home, it's the rare snack that's genuinely cheap, genuinely high in fiber, and still feels like something you'd eat during a movie. If you want a second DIY snack in that same spirit, [crispy roasted chickpeas](/crispy-roasted-chickpeas-high-fiber-snack/) scratch the crunchy-salty itch too, and dry chickpeas are one of the bags that [win the protein table and the fiber table at the same time](/beans-double-win-fiber-protein/).

## Are nuts and seeds worth it for fiber?

The cheap ones are. Dry roasted peanuts come in at 13.7 grams of fiber per dollar with a 16 oz jar at $2.78, and sunflower seed kernels land right behind at 13.1. Peanut butter holds its own at 11.4 grams per dollar, and the $4.97 jar is a 40 oz monster that lasts long enough to become a household member. That same jar is the outright winner of [the ranking of protein you don't have to cook](/no-cook-protein-per-dollar/). It also anchors these [no-bake oat and flax energy balls](/no-bake-high-fiber-energy-balls-recipe/), which turn pantry staples into something that feels like a bakery purchase.

Almonds are where the math turns on you. At $6.47 for a 16 oz bag, they manage 8.8 grams of fiber per dollar, behind bananas. Almonds are fine food with real fiber in them. They're just priced like a treasure, so buy them because you like them, not because a wellness post said they're a fiber snack.

## Is fresh fruit or dried fruit the better snack fiber deal?

Fresh wins in this sample. Bananas deliver 11.6 grams of fiber per dollar at $0.65 a pound, beating both prunes (8.1) and raisins (6.3), and they lead the fruit half of [the full fruit and vegetable ranking](/produce-fiber-per-dollar-ranked/) as well. Gala apples now carry an exact USDA match and land at 7.3 grams per dollar, which still clears raisins.

Drying fruit concentrates the sugar and the price faster than it concentrates the value. Raisins at $3.34 for a 20 oz canister are the clearest example: last place at 6.3 grams per dollar. Raisins aren't bad food. They're just an expensive place to shop for fiber.

## What does a smart high-fiber snack setup actually look like?

Three purchases, under $7 total by these prices: popcorn kernels ($2.28), a bag of carrots ($1.58), and a jar of peanuts ($2.78). That covers salty-crunchy, fresh-crunchy, and rich, though the popcorn and peanut rows rest on proxy nutrition matches rather than exact ones, so read their per-dollar figures as close estimates. Carrots get a serious upgrade from a batch of [homemade high-fiber hummus](/high-fiber-hummus-recipe-homemade/), and if your cravings show up after dinner, these [nighttime sweet tooth snack ideas](/healthy-sweet-tooth-snack-ideas-night/) lean on fruit and peanut butter rather than the cookie sleeve.

The bigger picture: snacks are where fiber budgets quietly die, because the default options are refined and priced for convenience.
