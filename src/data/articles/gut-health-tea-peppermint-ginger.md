Peppermint and ginger tea, made the way the recipe card does it: 1 cup of water, a 1-inch piece of fresh ginger sliced and bruised, and 1 tablespoon of fresh peppermint leaves or one peppermint tea bag. Simmer the ginger 5 to 7 minutes, take the pot off the heat, add the mint, cover, and steep another 5 to 7 minutes. Strain. Caffeine-free, about 10 to 20 cents a cup, and about 10 minutes from knife to mug.

That's the useful promise. Not a treatment, not a wellness ritual that requires a matching glass bottle, and not a reason to spend $18 on a tiny pouch with mountains printed on it. It's a warm drink that costs pennies. Too little ginger tastes like warm tap water. Too much peppermint tastes like liquid toothpaste.

## How to brew the cup

The card at the bottom of the page is the same cup. This is the version you can cook from without scrolling past a sermon.

1. **Slice and bruise a 1-inch piece of fresh ginger.** Scrub it and leave the skin on. Cut it into coins, then press the coins once with the side of the knife so they split a little. More surface, more flavor, less time waiting on one stubborn chunk.
2. **Simmer the ginger in 1 cup of water for 5 to 7 minutes.** A real simmer, not a rolling boil that beats the ginger into pulp. Five minutes is a lighter cup. Seven minutes bites harder.
3. **Take the pot off the heat and add the peppermint.** Use 1 tablespoon of fresh leaves, bruised once between your palms, or one plain peppermint tea bag. Don't boil the mint. Boiled mint goes dull, like the plant already gave up.
4. **Cover and steep 5 to 7 minutes, then strain.** A lid or a saucer is the whole trick. Peppermint's aroma leaves with the steam. An open mug gives the kitchen the best part while you drink what's left.
5. **Taste it plain before you add anything.** Lemon brightens the edges. A teaspoon of honey softens the ginger. Add either because you like it, not because a caption assigned the lemon a job.

If the cup is weak, change one thing. Another couple of ginger slices, or a few more mint leaves. Don't double the whole pot at once unless you enjoy troubleshooting a drink like a lab. If it's too strong, add hot water after you strain it.

A second mug is the same method with the amounts doubled: 2 cups of water, a 2-inch piece of ginger, 2 tablespoons of mint or two tea bags. Still simmer the ginger first. Still add the mint off the heat. If you bought a whole knob for one inch of it, keep the rest in the fridge or the freezer so next week's cup isn't a wrinkled souvenir from the crisper.

No saucepan? The pour-over in the diagram still works: 3 to 4 thin ginger coins, 1 teaspoon dried peppermint, about 12 ounces of just-boiled water, covered 5 to 10 minutes. The recipe card is the simmered cup. The diagram is the mug version.

## Who should skip it

> **GERD or a hiatal hernia.** Peppermint can relax the lower esophageal sphincter, the valve that's supposed to keep stomach acid down. [NCCIH says peppermint oil shouldn't be taken by people with a hernia or gastroesophageal reflux disease, especially at high doses](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/irritable-bowel-syndrome-what-you-need-to-know). That warning is about the oil, not a mug of leaves. If mint already gives you heartburn, skip the cup or brew the ginger alone.
>
> **Anticoagulants.** This is a slice of ginger in a cup, not a capsule. If you take a blood thinner, ask your doctor before ginger becomes a daily drink. [NCCIH's broader line is the same for any medicine](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ginger): talk with your provider before using ginger, because some herbs and medicines interact.
>
> **Pregnancy.** Ask your doctor before this is an everyday cup. [NCCIH says food amounts of oral peppermint are likely safe in pregnancy](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/peppermint-oil), and [says to ask your provider before using ginger while pregnant](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ginger). A prenatal plan is a conversation, not a tea recipe.

## What the IBS trials actually tested

Less than the tea aisle implies, and the gap is specific enough to say out loud.

The IBS trials are on enteric-coated peppermint-oil capsules, not tea. NCCIH points to a 2022 review of 10 studies covering 1,030 participants in which peppermint oil beat placebo for overall IBS symptoms and abdominal pain, and notes that the American College of Gastroenterology recommends peppermint oil for overall IBS symptoms ([Peppermint Oil: Usefulness and Safety](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/peppermint-oil)). Those capsules are concentrated oil, coated so more of it gets past the stomach ([NCCIH on IBS](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/irritable-bowel-syndrome-what-you-need-to-know)). NCCIH is blunt about the leaf: very little research has been done on peppermint leaf, and there isn't enough evidence to say whether peppermint leaf is useful for any health condition. A lot of tea articles quietly hand the capsule results to the tea bag. That's the move to watch for.

Ginger has the same shape of problem. NCCIH says most ginger studies tested dietary supplements rather than foods, that ginger may be helpful for nausea and vomiting associated with pregnancy, and that most motion sickness studies haven't shown it to help ([Ginger: Usefulness and Safety](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ginger)). A small 2008 trial in 24 healthy volunteers found that 1,200 mg of ginger in capsules sped up gastric emptying ([Effects of ginger on gastric emptying and motility in healthy humans](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18403946/)). Capsules, fasted, in a lab. Nobody has measured what a 1-inch piece leaves in the mug after you strain it.

So the honest position: warm, cheap, caffeine-free, possibly comfortable after dinner. Any gut benefit at tea strength is unproven. A mug of leaves and sliced root shouldn't borrow claims from concentrated products.

## Fresh leaves, a tea bag, or the dried jar

The recipe is written for fresh peppermint or one tea bag, because that's a Tuesday cup you can repeat. Fresh leaves taste the softest and look decent floating in a glass mug. They also wilt, and a pot of mint can take over a garden bed with the confidence of an invasive species. Buy fresh when you're already using mint for something else that week.

A plain peppermint tea bag wins when you don't want leaves in the sink. Check the ingredient panel. If peppermint is the only thing listed, you're buying the same leaf in a paper packet. That's a convenience charge, not a different plant.

Dried loose peppermint is the jar that makes sense if this becomes a habit. A teaspoon stands in for the tablespoon of fresh leaves in 1 cup of water. Keep it off the stove. Heat and steam flatten dried herbs long before the jar looks empty.

Fresh or frozen ginger beats ground ginger in this pot. Ground ginger sinks into a gritty layer and is much harder to pull back once the water tastes like a spice rack fell in. Slice a knob, freeze the coins flat, and drop them into the simmer when the fresh drawer looks bleak. One knob, stored cold, covers a lot of 1-inch pieces.

## What a cup costs

Homemade quietly wins the receipt. A $1 knob of fresh ginger can make roughly 8 to 10 cups, depending on how assertive you like the simmer. Dried peppermint costs a few dollars a jar and lasts for dozens of teaspoons. Even with a teaspoon of honey, a homemade cup generally lands around 10 to 20 cents.

Boxed "digestive" or "tummy" blends often cost 30 to 50 cents a bag. Some taste good. Read the label anyway, because the first two ingredients are frequently peppermint and ginger, followed by a parade of herbs that mostly make the box sound expensive. If you like that blend, buy it. Just know what the convenience costs.

There is one fair argument for tea bags. They reduce waste if fresh ginger and loose mint keep dying in your kitchen. The cheapest ingredient isn't cheap when you throw out half of it. Start with a small box, see whether the habit sticks, then buy the loose ingredients. A pantry should match the week you actually have, not the person you briefly became in the tea aisle.

For an office drawer, bags also win. Keep peppermint bags at work, bring a few frozen ginger slices in a small container, and skip the knife, the strainer, and the conversation with a curious coworker.

## What this tea won't do

Peppermint ginger tea won't replace a varied diet. If meals are low in fiber, the cup is a pleasant side habit, not the plan. Beans, oats, vegetables, fruit, and enough fluid do more of the everyday work. A bag of dry pintos returns 41.7 grams of fiber per dollar and old-fashioned oats 35.8 in the [fiber per dollar ranking](/fiber-per-dollar-cheapest-high-fiber-foods/).

It also won't erase a rough meal. Tea after loaded nachos is a peace treaty, not a pardon. The cup might feel comfortable. It doesn't make the portion smaller.

Very strong ginger can feel harsh on an empty stomach. [NCCIH lists abdominal discomfort, heartburn, diarrhea, and mouth and throat irritation as possible side effects of ginger taken orally](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ginger). Start with the 1-inch piece in the recipe, not half a root. If the cup burns rather than warms, add hot water. More intensity isn't proof that anything is working. Sometimes you've just made spicy water.

For advance prep, refrigerate strained tea in a sealed jar for up to three days. Leave lemon and honey out until you pour a cup, so each mug can go a different direction. The mint aroma fades in the fridge. Useful for tomorrow. Not an argument for a gallon.

The practical version still fits in one line: 1 cup of water, a 1-inch piece of ginger simmered 5 to 7 minutes, mint steeped covered off the heat for 5 to 7 minutes, then strain. That cup may feel good after dinner, costs less than the boxed version, and tastes like a decision. Good enough.
