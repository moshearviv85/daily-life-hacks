I had a dollar in my head and a bag of flour in the cart. The flour looked boring. The chicken looked like dinner. Then I asked the only question the receipt actually answers. What does that dollar buy in protein, and what does it buy in fiber?

The short protein list is one-dollar-protein-what-it-buys-2026.csv. The short fiber list is one-dollar-fiber-what-it-buys-2026.csv. Where a staple is missing from one of those lists, the gram comes from protein-per-dollar-2026.csv or fiber-per-dollar-2026.csv, same price, same USDA record. The filenames stay in the sentence. This page doesn't link the files. Building one day that clears both targets is a different question, on [what 50 grams of protein and 30 grams of fiber cost](/cost-of-50g-protein-and-30g-fiber-per-day/).

## What does $1 buy in protein vs. fiber at 2026 US grocery prices?

Whole wheat flour buys 96.0 grams of protein and 77.8 grams of fiber ([FDC 168893](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168893/nutrients)). Green split peas buy 73.9 and 71.0 ([FDC 172428](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/172428/nutrients)). Bone-in chicken drumsticks buy 50.3 grams of protein and 0 grams of fiber ([FDC 172373](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/172373/nutrients)). The protein Daily Value on the label is 50 grams. The fiber Daily Value is 28 grams. One dollar of flour clears both numbers, and you still have to bake it.

That's the comparison. Fifteen staples. Two nutrients. Prices that were already published.

## Fifteen staples, side by side

Protein is the orange bar. Fiber is the slate bar. The share of the Daily Value is one dollar of that nutrient divided by 50 grams or by 28 grams, then rounded half up to a whole percent. Where those two numbers come from is the [label Daily Values page](/fiber-protein-daily-values-explained/). This table is only the grocery dollar.

Chia seeds aren't in the protein file. [FDC 170554](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170554/nutrients) lists 16.54 grams of protein per 100 grams, and the fiber file prices a 32 ounce bag at $9.44. Run that through the bag and you get 15.9 grams of protein per dollar, rounded half up to one decimal. The 33.1 grams of fiber are the published row. I didn't pretend the 15.9 was already sitting in the protein file.

Drumstick fiber is 0.0 grams per 100 grams on that same USDA record, so the slate bar is a zero. The protein file already takes 33 percent off the bag for bone and cartilage. You're not paying for a drumstick and then counting the bone as dinner.

Navy beans, black beans, and pinto beans use the July 2026 BLS average. That's $3.38 for a 2 pound bag of navy or black beans, and $6.75 for a 4 pound bag of pintos. Drumsticks are a July 2026 Walmart bag. Flour, lentils, split peas, chickpeas, red lentils, spaghetti, peanut butter, oats, barley, canned black beans, and chia keep the store-check price, and those rows don't carry a month. I didn't relabel a blank cell as July. The rules live on the [methodology page](/methodology/).

Red lentils' 25.3 grams of fiber, peanut butter's 11.4, barley's 36.3 grams of protein, and the can's 30.1 grams of protein are in the long files, not in both short one-dollar lists. Same FDC id. Same package price. I didn't shop again.

## Split peas do both jobs

Flour wins the chart and then stares at you. Nobody eats flour with a spoon.

Green split peas are the row you can put in a pot. One dollar buys 73.9 grams of protein and 71.0 grams of fiber. Brown lentils beat them on protein, 77.7 grams, and lose on fiber, 33.7 grams ([FDC 172420](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/172420/nutrients)). If you want the quality dock, where a digestibility score walks the drumsticks past the lentil bag, that's [plant versus animal protein per dollar](/plant-vs-animal-protein-cost-per-dollar/). This page doesn't multiply by a score. Raw grams only.

Pearled barley flips the other way. Fiber is 57.1 grams per dollar and protein is 36.3 ([FDC 170284](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170284/nutrients)). That's a fiber food that happens to carry protein, which is why it leads the cookable grains on the [cheapest whole grains for fiber](/cheapest-whole-grains-for-fiber-per-dollar/) page and sits mid-pack here.

Peanut butter is the odd jar. 50.7 grams of protein and 11.4 grams of fiber ([FDC 174266](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/174266/nutrients)). One dollar is 101 percent of the protein Daily Value and 41 percent of the fiber Daily Value. Useful. Not a salad.

Canned black beans are the convenience tax. Dry black beans are 58.0 grams of protein and 41.6 grams of fiber. The can is 30.1 and 34.4 ([FDC 175188](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/175188/nutrients)). Same bean, more water, fewer grams per dollar. If the week is a desk drawer instead of a pot, the portable cut is [cheapest high-protein snacks you can carry](/cheapest-high-protein-snacks-and-portable-foods/).

## What the dollar doesn't buy

Oil, salt, the hour the lentils need, and the bone that already came off the drumstick math. A Daily Value percent isn't dinner. Flour at 192 percent of the protein value and 278 percent of the fiber value is a bag, not breakfast.

The wider ranking those rates come from is [protein per dollar adjusted for quality](/protein-per-dollar-adjusted-for-quality/). This page is only the question of what one dollar buys of each nutrient.

Fifteen foods. Two bars. The flour still needs a recipe before anyone calls it dinner.
