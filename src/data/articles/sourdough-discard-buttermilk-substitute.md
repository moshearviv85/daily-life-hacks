The recipe says one cup of buttermilk. The jar says sourdough discard. Those are not the same pour, and the cup line is how this goes wrong.

Buttermilk is liquid. It brings acid, water, and a little milk fat. A 100% hydration discard is half flour and half water by weight. Tip the jar to the cup line and you've added a scoop of flour the card didn't budget, then shorted the liquid by that same scoop. The pancakes go thick. The cornbread goes dry in the middle and smug about it.

## Can you substitute sourdough discard for buttermilk?

Yes, if you weigh the discard and fix the flour and the liquid. No, if you treat the cup as a 1:1 swap. Buttermilk adds zero flour. Discard doesn't.

The split is the one in the [sourdough discard calculator](/tools/sourdough-discard-calculator/). Hydration comes in as a percent and goes into the math as a ratio, h = percent / 100. Flour in the discard is W / (1 + h). Water in the discard is W * h / (1 + h). W is the discard weight. At 100%, h is 1, so the scoop is half flour and half water. At 80%, h is 0.8, which is 4 parts water to 5 parts flour, and you divide by 1.8.

Weigh discard equal to the buttermilk weight. Subtract that flour from the recipe flour. The water covers only part of the buttermilk, so the rest is thinning liquid: milk or water, not another glug of buttermilk. At 100% the flour you subtract and the liquid you add are the same number. At 80% both of those numbers are bigger, because more of the scoop is flour.

## The buttermilk conversion table

One cup of lowfat cultured buttermilk is 245 grams ([FDC 170874](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170874/nutrients)). This site's cup of stirred 100% discard is 227 grams, the King Arthur cup the calculator stores as CUP_GRAMS. Don't mix those cups. The table starts from the buttermilk, then asks you to weigh that many grams of discard. A bubbly jar lies to a measuring cup, which is the whole point of the [gram measuring guide](/how-to-measure-sourdough-discard-grams/).

Printed grams are rounded to the nearest tenth, the same way the calculator prints them. 30.6 and 30.6 don't add back to 61.25 on the nose. That's the tenth, not a second formula.

| Buttermilk | Hydration | Discard weight | Flour to subtract | Water in the discard | Thinning liquid |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1/4 cup (61.25 g) | 100% (1:1) | 61.25 g | 30.6 g | 30.6 g | 30.6 g |
| 1/4 cup (61.25 g) | 80% (4:5) | 61.25 g | 34 g | 27.2 g | 34 g |
| 1/2 cup (122.5 g) | 100% (1:1) | 122.5 g | 61.3 g | 61.3 g | 61.3 g |
| 1/2 cup (122.5 g) | 80% (4:5) | 122.5 g | 68.1 g | 54.4 g | 68.1 g |
| 3/4 cup (183.75 g) | 100% (1:1) | 183.75 g | 91.9 g | 91.9 g | 91.9 g |
| 3/4 cup (183.75 g) | 80% (4:5) | 183.75 g | 102.1 g | 81.7 g | 102.1 g |
| 1 cup (245 g) | 100% (1:1) | 245 g | 122.5 g | 122.5 g | 122.5 g |
| 1 cup (245 g) | 80% (4:5) | 245 g | 136.1 g | 108.9 g | 136.1 g |

Read one cup at 100% straight across. 245 grams of discard, subtract 122.5 grams of flour, and the discard already holds 122.5 grams of water, so you add 122.5 grams of milk or water. At 80% that same 245 gram scoop is 136.1 grams of flour and 108.9 grams of water, and the thinning pour is 136.1 grams. Stiffer than 80%, or a 50% dough that looks like spackle, isn't this table. The [hydration conversion chart](/sourdough-discard-hydration-conversion-chart/) splits 100%, 80%, and 50% at the gram weights the calculator already uses.

## Baking soda still needs that acid

The subtraction keeps the flour and the water. The lift is a different ingredient.

Buttermilk sits near pH 4.5 ([America's Test Kitchen](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/articles/1212-all-about-acid-cooking-s-most-versatile-ingredient)). Baking soda needs an acid to make carbon dioxide, and that's the foam. Discard brings lactic acid and acetic acid from the jar, not from milk. Andrew Janjigian puts a typical discard near pH 3.5, sourer than that buttermilk ([Wordloaf](https://wordloaf.substack.com/p/wordloafs-rules-for-sourdough-discard)). [Lau et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8306212/) measured unfed sourdough at pH 4.0 to 4.8, so a milder jar can sit right next to buttermilk and a neglected one can run closer to 3.5 to 4.0. Either one reacts with baking soda. Don't walk off. The foam starts when the soda hits the jar.

What the jar doesn't bring is milk fat or milk solids. Buttermilk's tenderness lives there. Acid can stand in. Butterfat can't. If the recipe also uses baking powder, the powder already carries its own acid, and the discard is mostly flavor plus the flour and water you just subtracted. The [no-yeast discard recipes](/sourdough-discard-recipes-no-yeast/) separate those two leaveners on purpose.

## Where the swap works

Pancakes, waffles, cornbread, and drop biscuits. Wet batters. A little tang reads as the buttermilk the card was chasing, and nobody is asking for paper-thin flakes of butter.

The [beginner pancakes](/easy-sourdough-discard-recipes-beginners/) already put baking soda into a cup of discard, and the batter foams on contact. That's the reaction you're borrowing. A waffle batch on the no-yeast list is the same soda hit at a bigger gram weight. Cornbread and drop biscuits are scoop batters. Do the flour subtraction, add the thinning liquid, and get them on the heat. They can survive a jar that isn't a dairy aisle.

Stir the discard first. Weigh it. If the jar is 80% instead of 100%, use that row. Pretending a stiff scoop is pourable is how the batter turns into cement.

## Where it fails

Tender layer cakes. The missing milk fat is the soft crumb, and a sourer jar than pH 4.5 can push the soda harder than the formula expected. Extra flour is easy to mis-weigh even after the table tells you what to subtract. A fine cake keeps that grudge. I wouldn't run this swap into a birthday layer cake.

Flaky cut-in biscuits fail for a different reason. They're a butter architecture, not a smoothie. Cold butter stays in shards only if the liquid is small and cold. Thick discard plus a thinning splash changes that ratio, and the flakes slump into a scone that didn't mean to be one. Drop biscuits can take this swap. Cut-in biscuits can't.

A recipe already written for discard is not this problem. The biscuits on the no-yeast page have their own grams, their own powder, and their own clock. Follow that card. Don't pour a jar into a classic buttermilk formula and hope the cup line forgives you.

Weigh the buttermilk, weigh that much discard, subtract the flour, thin the liquid, and get the pan hot while the soda still cares. The pancake foams. The layer cake notices the missing butterfat. Use the jar where a quick batter can take the tang.
