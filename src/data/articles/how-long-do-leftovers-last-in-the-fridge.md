Three to four days. USDA's [leftovers guidance](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/leftovers-and-food-safety) is unambiguous: leftovers can be kept in the refrigerator for 3 to 4 days, or frozen. And you have two hours from the moment the food comes off the heat to get it cold, which is the part most people get wrong.

The container that's been in there since Sunday is not a judgment call. It's Wednesday.

| Leftover | Refrigerator | Freezer |
|---|---|---|
| Cooked meat or poultry | 3 to 4 days | 2 to 6 months |
| Soups and stews | 3 to 4 days | 2 to 3 months |
| Pizza | 3 to 4 days | 1 to 2 months |
| Chicken nuggets or patties | 3 to 4 days | 1 to 3 months |
| Egg, chicken, tuna, ham, macaroni salads | 3 to 4 days | Does not freeze well |
| Cooked rice and pasta | 3 to 4 days | Not listed |

*Source: [USDA FoodSafety.gov Cold Food Storage Chart](https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/cold-food-storage-charts), reviewed September 2023, plus the rice and pasta line from [USDA FSIS Shelf-Stable Food Safety](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/shelf-stable-food). Refrigerator at 40F or below, freezer at 0F or below. Freezer times are for quality only.*

## Where the sources disagree

On the fridge, everybody agrees: 3 to 4 days, from FSIS, from FoodSafety.gov, and from [Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/food-safety/leftovers).

On the freezer, they don't. FSIS's leftovers page says 3 to 4 months. The FoodSafety.gov chart gives cooked meat or poultry 2 to 6 months and pizza 1 to 2. Illinois Extension follows FSIS at 3 to 4 months.

Neither number is a safety cliff. Both sources repeat the same underlying rule, which is that freezer times are quality guidelines because food held constantly at 0F stays safe indefinitely. If you want one number to plan around, 3 months is the conservative read and nothing bad happens at month four except a duller dinner.

## How fast do leftovers need to get cold?

Within two hours of cooking, or one hour if the room is above 90F. FSIS says perishable food left at room temperature longer than that should be thrown away.

The reason is the [danger zone](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/danger-zone-40f-140f), which is the range between 40F and 140F where bacteria grow most rapidly, doubling in number in as little as 20 minutes.

FSIS also calls improper cooling of cooked food one of the most common causes of foodborne illness, and gives a specific fix: divide large amounts into shallow containers so they cool fast. A big pot of soup takes forever to come down in one piece. Split into four containers, it's cold in half an hour.

And no, you don't have to wait for it to cool first. The FDA says [putting hot food in the refrigerator doesn't harm the appliance](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/refrigerator-thermometers-cold-facts-about-food-safety), and FSIS says hot food can be placed directly in the refrigerator or chilled in an ice bath first.

## How do you reheat them safely?

To 165F, checked with a thermometer. FSIS says to reheat leftovers to 165F and to bring sauces, soups and gravies to a rolling boil, keeping food covered so it heats all the way through.

Microwaves earn a specific warning. FSIS says to cover and rotate the food, check the temperature in several places, and allow resting time, because microwaves leave cold spots. Dense food like a thick casserole keeps cooking longer than bread or small vegetables do.

You can also reheat frozen leftovers without thawing them at all. FSIS says that's safe, it just takes longer.

## What goes wrong in a real fridge

Two things, and neither is exotic.

The first is that you can't tell. FSIS notes that spoilage bacteria and the bacteria that cause illness are different populations, and Illinois Extension makes the point directly: bacteria that cause illness can grow without changing the odor, color, or texture of the food. Smelling the container is not a test.

The second is that nothing is labeled. Illinois Extension recommends labeling leftovers with the name of the food and the current date, and storing the container above all raw food that still needs cooking so nothing drips down. Two seconds with a marker removes the entire argument.

* **Shallow containers, not the stockpot.** Faster cooling, easier stacking.
* **Date the lid.** Day three is a reheat, day five is a bin.
* **Freeze on day one, not day three.** FSIS says to freeze leftovers within 3 to 4 days, so freezing early keeps the quality you actually paid for.
