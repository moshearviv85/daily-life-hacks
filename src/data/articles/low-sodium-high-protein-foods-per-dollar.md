You can buy a cheap protein and still salt the day without noticing. The dry-bean bag is quiet. The seasoned can, the loaf, and the cheese aren't. I wanted one list that had to clear both tests at once: enough protein per dollar to matter, and sodium at or under the FDA's low-sodium number.

Twenty-five foods do it. Whole wheat flour leads at 96.0 g of protein per dollar with 2 mg of sodium per 100 g ([FDC 168893](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168893/nutrients)). Dry brown lentils are the first food on the list that eats like dinner: 77.7 g per dollar, 24.63 g of protein per 100 g, and 6 mg of sodium ([FDC 172420](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/172420/nutrients)). [21 CFR 101.61](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-D/section-101.61) is where FDA sets the low-sodium line at 140 mg or less per reference amount. This page uses 140 mg per 100 g so a bag of lentils and a chicken breast share one ruler.

## Which cheap grocery foods are both high in protein and low in sodium?

Twenty-five foods. Each one has at least 9.9 g of protein per 100 g and no more than 140 mg of sodium per 100 g. Orange bars on the chart are under 20 mg. Slate bars still clear 140 mg, they're just not almost salt-free.

## Why flour wins and dinner starts at lentils

Flour is the spreadsheet champion and a bad answer to "what's for dinner." A 5 lb bag was $3.12 in the audited Walmart Great Value price, and the protein in that flour is 13.21 g per 100 g. You still have to bake it into something. If the plan is a bowl, start at lentils.

The foods people mean by cheap protein are the next rows, and they're almost saltless. Green split peas are 73.9 g per dollar and 5 mg of sodium ([FDC 172428](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/172428/nutrients)), from a 16 oz bag at $1.42. Navy beans are 59.9 g and 5 mg, black beans are 58.0 g and 5 mg, and pintos are 57.6 g and 12 mg. That's the 57.6 to 59.9 band, and the sodium on those three is 5 to 12 mg per 100 g. Chickpeas (56.7 g, 24 mg) and red lentils (56.0 g, 7 mg) sit right behind them.

A 16 oz bag of brown lentils was $1.44. That's the whole trick. The cheapest protein in the store is also the lowest sodium, as long as you're willing to cook it. The [plant-protein ranking](/plant-based-protein-sources-complete-guide/) prices those bags on their own. This page only keeps the ones that also clear the sodium line. If you'd rather see the wider protein list, including foods that didn't make 140 mg, it's the [protein per dollar ranking](/protein-per-dollar-adjusted-for-quality/).

Dry weight is doing some of the bragging. These bean numbers are the food as purchased. Cook them and they drink water, so 100 g on the plate holds less protein and less sodium than 100 g in the bag. Compare a dry number to a cooked number and you'll invent a winner. [Cooking dried beans](/how-to-cook-dried-beans-from-scratch/) is the pot that turns the bag into dinner. [Canned versus dried beans](/canned-beans-vs-dried-beans-nutrition/) is the price gap if the can is what you'll actually open.

## The chicken, yogurt, and tofu end of the list

Meat shows up, and it isn't the top. Bone-in chicken drumsticks are the first animal food, at 50.3 g of protein per dollar and 106 mg of sodium per 100 g ([FDC 172373](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/172373/nutrients)). The price note on that row is easy to skip: USDA's refuse factor takes off 33 percent for bone and cartilage, and the edible fraction on the protein sheet is 0.67. The 50.3 g is the meat, not the bone you throw away. The bag in the price file was 5 lb for $5.46.

Boneless skinless thighs are 27.7 g per dollar and 95 mg of sodium ([FDC 173627](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173627/nutrients)). Boneless skinless breast is 24.6 g and 45 mg ([FDC 171077](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/171077/nutrients)). Breast has more protein per 100 g, 22.5 g against 19.66 g in the thigh, and it costs more per dollar of protein. Both clear 140 mg. Neither catches a bag of lentils.

Plain nonfat Greek yogurt is the quiet dairy win: 27.5 g per dollar, 10.19 g of protein per 100 g, 36 mg of sodium, from a 32 oz tub at $3.36 ([FDC 170894](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170894/nutrients)). Sunflower kernels land at 31.7 g per dollar and 9 mg of sodium ([FDC 170562](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170562/nutrients)). Frozen shelled edamame is 18.0 g and 6 mg ([FDC 168410](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168410/nutrients)). Extra-firm tofu is last among the foods I'd actually call a protein staple: 13.6 g per dollar, 9.98 g per 100 g, and 4 mg of sodium ([FDC 174290](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/174290/nutrients)), a 14 oz block at $2.92. Tofu barely clears the 9.9 g protein floor. It's here because 4 mg of sodium is almost nothing, not because it beats beans on price.

Almonds are the humblebrag of the table. One milligram of sodium per 100 g, and 14.8 g of protein per dollar. Great snack, bad bargain next to a $1.44 bag of lentils.

## What misses the 140 mg line

Eggs are the one that feels unfair. [FDC 171287](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/171287/nutrients) puts a raw whole egg at 142 mg of sodium per 100 g. The protein sheet has them at 12.56 g of protein per 100 g. Two milligrams over a round-number rule doesn't make eggs a salt bomb. It does keep them off a list that promised the FDA line.

The foods that blow past it are the processed ones, which is the same story as the [56-food sodium ranking](/low-sodium-budget-foods-ranked/). Commercially prepared whole wheat bread is 455 mg per 100 g on [FDC 172688](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/172688/nutrients), with 12.45 g of protein per 100 g. The flour that bread started as is row 1 of this table, at 2 mg. Canned kidney beans are 296 mg per 100 g on [FDC 173741](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173741/nutrients), and 5.22 g of protein per 100 g, so they miss the protein floor too. Cheese, bacon, and canned fish sit even higher on the sodium sheet. Cheap and salty is a processing story. It isn't the dry-goods aisle.

If the food is already under 140 mg and dinner still tastes flat, the salt shaker is a choice, not a requirement. [Citrus, herbs, and umami](/big-flavor-less-salt-citrus-herbs-umami-swaps/) covers the swaps that aren't another pinch.

## Where these numbers come from

The protein sheet has 49 foods. Thirty-six of them have a sodium row with the same USDA record id. Twenty-five of those 36 are at or under 140 mg per 100 g and at or above 9.9 g of protein per 100 g. The 9.9 g floor is this page's screen, not an FDA "high protein" claim. Pearled barley is the edge case at 9.91 g per 100 g. Tofu is 9.98 g. Anything under that floor never entered the table, even when the sodium was tiny.

Prices are the audited basket already in those two files: Walmart and BLS figures, mostly July 2026, inherited rather than shopped again for this page. Sodium and protein are USDA FoodData Central values for the form named on the row. Dry beans are dry. Chicken is raw. The rules for edible portion and the BLS series are on the [methodology page](/methodology/).

A dollar of fiber is a different shopping list. Frozen peas and bagged carrots lead that one, and the ranking is [cheapest high-fiber fruits and vegetables](/cheapest-high-fiber-fruits-and-vegetables/). Same habit, other nutrient: buy the boring bag, cook it, and let the seasoned stuff be the accent instead of the base.
