Sheet pan dinners are popular for a good reason. The whole meal goes on one tray, into a hot oven, and when the timer rings you have dinner and exactly one pan to scrub. Line it right and it's zero. Here's [when to use parchment vs silicone liners](/baking-sheet-liners-parchment-silicone-when-to-use/) so the fish doesn't glue itself to the metal.

This sheet pan salmon and vegetables recipe is for the nights when flipping things with a spatula feels impossible. Vegetables go in first, salmon joins ten minutes later, and about 30 minutes in you have flaky fish and tender veggies.
## Why This Combo Actually Works

Salmon cooks fast and doesn't need much beyond olive oil, salt, and a little dried herb. Broccoli, zucchini, and cherry tomatoes roast happily at the same high heat. Salmon also shows up in our [plain-English selenium foods guide](/selenium-containing-foods-easy-ways/) alongside eggs, chicken, and beans. Dinner doesn't need a mineral-themed costume to count.

Starting the vegetables 10 minutes early is what makes the timing work. The broccoli gets crispy edges right as the salmon finishes. Send everything in at once and you get raw vegetables or dry fish. The same staggering runs [one-pan lemon herb chicken and veggies](/quick-dinner-recipes/) when you want poultry.
## Picking the Right Salmon and Veggies

Look for skin-on fillets. The skin shields the flesh from the hot metal, holds the fish together, and lifts away on the plate. Buy fillets of similar thickness so nobody gets a dry piece while someone else gets a raw one.

Farmed or wild matters more than most recipes admit, and it's why calorie counts for this dish vary so wildly online. USDA FoodData Central puts [farmed Atlantic salmon](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/175167/nutrients) at 208 calories per 100 grams raw and [sockeye](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173691/nutrients) at 131. Same pan, same timing, about 120 calories a serving apart.

| Raw salmon, per 100 g | Calories | Protein | Fat | EPA + DHA | Vitamin D |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Atlantic, farmed | 208 | 20.4 g | 13.4 g | 1.96 g | 11.0 mcg |
| Sockeye, wild | 131 | 22.2 g | 4.69 g | 0.72 g | 14.1 mcg |

The 445-calorie figure here assumes a 5.5 oz farmed fillet plus its share of the vegetables and oil. Swap in sockeye and you're closer to 325.

Both sit on the FDA and EPA [Best Choices list](https://www.epa.gov/choose-fish-and-shellfish-wisely/epa-fda-advice-about-eating-fish-and-shellfish), which advises 2 to 3 servings of fish a week. NIH names fatty fish among the best natural food sources of [vitamin D](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminD-Consumer/), where the daily amount for adults 19 to 70 is 15 mcg. One 5 to 6 oz fillet lands right around that. On omega-3s, NIH stays careful: eating fatty fish as part of a healthy pattern [may support heart health](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-Consumer/), and no recommended EPA and DHA intake has been set. Dinner, not medicine.

## Keeping the Flavor Simple

There's no glaze or reduction sauce here unless you want the extra dishes. If a glaze is the point, cube the fish and make [air fryer salmon bites in garlic and honey](/air-fryer-salmon-bites-garlic-honey-glaze/), where the sauce goes on after cooking. A hefty squeeze of lemon out of the oven is usually all this fish needs, though a dollop of plain Greek yogurt, Dijon, or leftover pesto works too. One pan, one timer, one less decision today.

## Use a large enough sheet pan

A standard half-sheet pan, roughly 13 by 18 inches, gives four fillets and a pound of vegetables room to roast. Stack them in a damp mound and they steam. You wanted browned broccoli, not a tray of apologetic florets.

Only have a smaller sheet? Use two. Vegetables on one, salmon on the other, fish into the oven ten minutes later. An extra pan beats pretending overcrowding doesn't exist.

Parchment makes cleanup easier. Foil works, though fish sticks to it unless you oil it. A silicone mat insulates the bottom and can reduce browning. The [liner guide](/baking-sheet-liners-parchment-silicone-when-to-use/) covers the tradeoff.

## Dry the salmon before seasoning

Pat each fillet dry with a paper towel, then rub it lightly with oil and season. Surface moisture has to evaporate before browning starts, and drying keeps the herbs and garlic powder on the fish instead of sliding into a pink puddle.

A thin tail piece finishes minutes before a thick center cut. If the package hands you one odd skinny piece, fold its narrow end underneath, or pull it from the pan early.

Frozen salmon needs thawing first, and USDA lists [three safe methods](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/big-thaw-safe-defrosting-methods): the refrigerator, cold water in a leak-proof bag, or the microwave. Never the counter. A pound of seafood can thaw in cold water in an hour or less. Dry the fillets well afterward, because cooking from frozen dumps water onto the pan and wrecks the vegetable timing.

## How to tell when salmon is done

Start checking after eight minutes on the pan. Press the thickest part with a fork; the layers should separate under light pressure and the center shouldn't look raw. The [USDA safe minimum internal temperature](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/safe-temperature-chart) for fish and shellfish is 145 degrees F, measured in the thickest section without touching the pan.

Plenty of recipes tell you to pull salmon at 120 degrees F for a softer, medium-rare center. That's a texture preference, not a safety endorsement, and it isn't the number USDA publishes. Cooking for kids, older adults, anyone pregnant, or anyone immunocompromised? Use 145.

Carryover heat keeps cooking the fish after it leaves the oven, so pull thin fillets as soon as they're ready. Holding everything in until the thickest piece is done is how the smallest fillet becomes expensive pink chalk.

Numbers worth taping inside a cabinet door, from USDA's [leftovers guidance](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/leftovers-and-food-safety) and the [FoodSafety.gov cold storage chart](https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/cold-food-storage-charts).

| Checkpoint | Number |
|---|---|
| Fish, safe internal temperature | 145 degrees F |
| Raw salmon in the fridge | 1 to 3 days |
| Leftovers refrigerated within | 2 hours |
| Cooked leftovers keep | 3 to 4 days |
| Reheat leftovers to | 165 degrees F |

## Vegetable swaps that keep the timing intact

Asparagus, green beans, sliced bell peppers, and small cauliflower florets fit the same schedule. Dense vegetables need a longer head start: potatoes, carrots, winter squash, and thick Brussels sprouts generally want 20 to 30 minutes before the salmon joins them.

Cut everything to a similar size and coat it lightly, not lavishly. Too much oil makes the tomatoes burst into a slick sauce that softens everything nearby.

## Store and reheat leftovers gently

Get leftovers into the fridge in a shallow, covered container within 2 hours of cooking, and eat them within 3 to 4 days. That's the USDA window, not a vibe. Store the salmon and vegetables separately if you can, so you can reheat the vegetables hard without turning the fish into an eraser.

Warm salmon in a covered skillet over low heat, or in short microwave bursts at reduced power, until it hits the 165 degrees F USDA gives for reheating leftovers. A spoonful of water makes gentle steam. Cold leftover salmon flaked over greens with lemon and mustard skips reheating entirely and makes tomorrow's lunch look intentional.
