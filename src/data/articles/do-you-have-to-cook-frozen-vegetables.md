Yes, cook them. Frozen vegetables are blanched before freezing, which is a brief heat step rather than a finished cook, and [Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/be-smart-eat-well-get-healthy/2017-04-11-cook-frozen-vegetables) advises heating any frozen vegetable before adding it to something cold like a salad. The bag is an ingredient, not a snack.

This surprises people, and I get why. The peas look done. They're bright green, they're soft, they came from a factory. Nothing about them says raw.

## Are frozen vegetables cooked before freezing?

Only partly. The step is blanching, and the [National Center for Home Food Preservation](https://nchfp.uga.edu/how/freeze/general-freezing-information/blanching-vegetables/) defines it as scalding vegetables in boiling water or steam for a short time, done to stop the enzyme actions that cause loss of flavor, color and texture. NCHFP notes blanching also helps remove dirt and microorganisms.

[University of Minnesota Extension](https://extension.umn.edu/preserving-and-preparing/how-blanch-vegetables-safe-preservation) is more direct about the safety half: fresh vegetables should be blanched before freezing to kill bacteria that might be on them, and many bacteria are destroyed at the temperature of boiling water or steam.

Look at the times, though. NCHFP's blanching table, which we walk through in [how to freeze fresh vegetables at home](/how-to-freeze-fresh-vegetables-at-home/), gives 3 minutes for green beans, 1.5 minutes for green peas, 2 minutes for sliced carrots. That is not dinner. That is a quick scald designed to switch off enzymes, and it's why the same guidance says frozen vegetables need less cooking time than fresh ones rather than none at all.

## Why does freezing not make them safe to eat as is?

Because freezing presses pause, it does not clean house.

USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service is unusually clear about this in its [freezing and food safety guidance](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/freezing-and-food-safety): freezing to 0F inactivates any microbes present in food, but "once thawed, however, these microbes can again become active, multiplying under the right conditions to levels that can lead to foodborne illness." FSIS says you must then handle thawed items as you would any perishable food.

UMN Extension says the same thing from the preservation side: freezing temporarily stops bacteria growth, and as the vegetables thaw, bacteria can begin to grow again.

So the frozen bag is safe while it's frozen. The question is what happens in the twenty minutes after it stops being frozen.

## Can frozen vegetables make you sick?

It's rare, and it has happened. Illinois Extension wrote its frozen vegetable guidance in the middle of a recall over possible Listeria contamination, and its summary is worth quoting on the mechanism: Listeria "is unusual in that it can grow in refrigerated temperatures that would inhibit growth of other bacteria," and it can live in a biofilm on surfaces like drains and processing equipment.

The FDA makes the same point about home refrigerators in its [refrigerator thermometer guidance](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/refrigerator-thermometers-cold-facts-about-food-safety), which is why it recommends wiping up spills promptly.

None of this is a reason to avoid the freezer aisle. It's a reason to run the bag through heat. Illinois Extension gives the number: 155F kills it.

## What does this change in a real kitchen?

Almost nothing, except for the cold dishes.

Hot uses are already fine. Soup, chili, stir fry, a sheet pan, a casserole, anything that hits real heat. That covers most of what a bag of frozen vegetables is for, and [cooking them without the mush](/how-to-cook-frozen-vegetables-without-the-mush/) is the harder problem.

The uses to fix are the cold ones. Frozen peas stirred straight into a pasta salad. Frozen corn scattered over a [black bean salsa](/quick-black-bean-and-corn-salsa-no-cooking-required/). Frozen edamame thrown in a grain bowl to defrost on the way to work. All of those want a two-minute cook and a cool-down first.

* **Microwave for two minutes, spread on a plate, chill.** Fastest fix, works for peas, corn, and edamame.
* **Boil for a minute, then ice water.** Better color, one more pot to wash.
* **Read the bag.** If a package says the product is ready to eat, that's the manufacturer taking responsibility. Most vegetable bags carry cooking directions instead.

And if the bag has been sitting on the counter thawing while you decided, that's a separate call. FSIS says to handle thawed items like any perishable food, and we walk through the refreezing rules in [can you refreeze frozen vegetables after thawing](/can-you-refreeze-frozen-vegetables-after-thawing/).
