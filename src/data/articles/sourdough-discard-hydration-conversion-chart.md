You poured a stiff scoop into a pancake card written for a pourable jar, and the batter turned into spackle. That's a hydration problem, not a bad jar. This sourdough discard hydration conversion chart is the subtraction: how much flour and water are already in 100%, 80%, and 50% discard, before you add another gram of either.

The formulas are the ones in the [sourdough discard calculator](/tools/sourdough-discard-calculator/). Hydration comes in as a percent and goes into the math as a ratio, h = percent / 100. Flour in the discard is D / (1 + h). Water in the discard is D * h / (1 + h). I didn't round the formula. I rounded the printed grams to the nearest tenth, the same way that calculator does.

## How do you convert 100%, 80%, and 50% discard?

**100% is 1:1.** Equal flour and water. Half the scoop is flour and half is water. A 100 gram pour is 50 grams of each.

**80% is 4:5.** Four parts water to five parts flour, so h is 0.8 and you divide the scoop by 1.8. A 100 gram scoop is 55.6 grams of flour and 44.4 grams of water.

**50% stiff is 1:2.** One part water to two parts flour. Two-thirds of the weight is flour. A 100 gram scoop is 66.7 grams of flour and 33.3 grams of water. That's the jar that looks like dough and wrecks a thin batter if you pretend it's the pourable one.

Subtract both numbers from the recipe. Leave the flour in and you've doubled it. Leave the water in and the dough goes slack. The [amount planner](/what-to-make-with-sourdough-discard-by-amount/) matches a jar to a recipe weight. This page matches the jar's thickness to the flour and water already hiding in that weight.

## The hydration conversion chart

Five weights, three hydrations. 50 grams, 100 grams, 150 grams, 200 grams, and 227 grams. That last line is one cup on this site. 100% is the 1:1 column, 80% is the 4:5 column, and 50% is the stiff 1:2 column.

| 50 g | 25 g | 25 g | 27.8 g | 22.2 g | 33.3 g | 16.7 g |
| 100 g | 50 g | 50 g | 55.6 g | 44.4 g | 66.7 g | 33.3 g |
| 150 g | 75 g | 75 g | 83.3 g | 66.7 g | 100 g | 50 g |

Read across one row and the flour plus the water comes back to the discard. 55.6 and 44.4 are 100. 126.1 and 100.9 are 227. If a tenth looks off, it's the same rounding the calculator uses, not a second formula.

## What one cup weighs, and which flour those grams are

CUP_GRAMS = 227. That's this site's cup, from [King Arthur Baking's 227 gram cup](https://www.kingarthurbaking.com/recipes/sourdough-starter-recipe), and it's what the calculator multiplies when you type cups. A stirred 100% cup is 113.5 grams of flour and 113.5 grams of water. A stiff 227 gram scoop is 151.3 grams of flour and 75.7 grams of water. Same cup label, different dough. Weigh it. A bubbly jar lies to a measuring cup, which is the whole point of the [gram measuring guide](/how-to-measure-sourdough-discard-grams/).

The flour those grams represent is a USDA FoodData Central weight, per 100 grams of flour, not a mystery scoop. Enriched unbleached all-purpose is [FDC 168936](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168936/nutrients), 364 kcal and 10.33 grams of protein per 100 grams. Whole-grain wheat flour is [FDC 168893](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168893/nutrients), 340 kcal and 13.21 grams of protein per 100 grams. The measuring guide already ran the all-purpose calorie line for two of these hydrations: about 182 kcal for 100 grams at 100%, and about 243 kcal for 100 grams at 50%, because the stiff scoop is two-thirds flour. An 80% jar sits between them. Use the flour grams in the chart, then the flour record you actually poured.

## How much discard can the recipe take?

There's a ceiling. The most discard a recipe can absorb is the smaller of two limits: the flour in the recipe times (1 + h), and the replaceable liquid times (1 + h) divided by h. Written the way the calculator writes it, that's min(F * (1 + h), W * (1 + h) / h). At 0% hydration there is no liquid cap, because the scoop is all flour. You won't meet a 0% jar in a normal kitchen. Past the cap, the "flour to add" or the "water to add" goes negative, which is a polite way of saying the recipe ran out of one of them.

Two cases are already on the calculator, so you can check this page against the tool.

100 grams at 100% into a recipe with 300 grams of flour and 210 grams of liquid. The discard holds 50 grams of flour and 50 grams of water. You still add 250 grams of flour and 160 grams of liquid. The recipe can take 420 grams of that discard before one of those additions hits zero.

150 grams at 50% into 250 grams of flour and 120 grams of liquid. The discard holds 100 grams of flour and 50 grams of water. You still add 150 grams of flour and 70 grams of liquid. The cap is 360 grams. The liquid limit is the one that binds.

An 80% example, same rounding. 100 grams of 80% discard into 260 grams of flour and 120 grams of liquid holds 55.6 grams of flour and 44.4 grams of water. You still add 204.4 grams of flour and 75.6 grams of liquid. The cap is 270 grams, and again it's the liquid that runs out first. If the recipe's "liquid" is milk, oil, and an egg, only subtract from the part you can actually replace. Oil isn't water. An egg isn't a glass of milk.

## Baking powder, baking soda, and no-yeast quick breads

The subtraction keeps the written ratio. It doesn't keep the rise. Unfed discard is a flavor scoop. If the loaf was counting on a ripe starter, this math won't grow you one. That's the difference on [discard versus active starter](/sourdough-discard-vs-active-starter/).

Quick breads bring their own leavening. [Beginner pancakes](/easy-sourdough-discard-recipes-beginners/) use baking soda, and the batter foams the second the soda hits the acid in the jar. Don't walk off. The [no-yeast cinnamon rolls](/sourdough-discard-cinnamon-rolls-no-yeast/) use baking powder and baking soda together, and the pan goes in as soon as the dough is wet, because the soda doesn't wait on your schedule. The [thin pizza crust](/easy-sourdough-discard-pizza-dough-no-yeast/) uses baking powder and skips the yeast rise. The rest of that no-yeast list, flatbread through banana bread, is [discard recipes with no added yeast](/sourdough-discard-recipes-no-yeast/).

Stiff, 80%, or pourable, the rule doesn't change. Match the hydration, subtract the flour and the water, and let the powder or the soda do the lifting. A ripe jar is a different recipe.

Weigh the scoop, read the row, and get the pan in while the soda still cares. That's the chart.
