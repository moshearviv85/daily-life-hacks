We all want that perfect, golden brown crust on a piece of chicken or a beautifully seared vegetable. And we all hate it when half that crust sticks to the bottom of the pan like glue. The secret to fixing both problems is incredibly boring, but it works. You have to learn how to preheat a skillet properly.

Putting food into a cold or lukewarm pan is the most common cooking mistake happening in kitchens every night. A cold pan gives the proteins in your food plenty of time to grab onto the metal before anything browns. A properly preheated pan sears the outside fast, and the crust that forms is what eventually lets go of the surface.

I used to just turn the burner on, splash some oil in, and throw the chicken in ten seconds later. The result was always gray, sad looking meat that tore when I tried to flip it. Taking three extra minutes to preheat the pan changed everything about how my food tasted.

## The rules of the preheat

The first rule is patience. You can't rush physics. Put your empty pan on the stove over medium heat. Not high heat. High heat gives you a pan that's scorching in the center and cold at the edges, because the metal can't spread that energy sideways as fast as the burner delivers it. Medium heat lets the temperature climb steadily and even out across the whole surface.

A heavy cast iron skillet takes about five minutes. The reason isn't mystical: there's a lot of metal there, and all of it has to absorb heat before the cooking surface stops changing temperature. That same mass is why cast iron barely flinches when cold food hits it. Cast iron also conducts heat far more slowly than aluminum or copper, so the spot over the burner runs ahead of the rim and the extra minutes are what let the two meet in the middle. A stainless steel pan is thinner and gets there in two or three minutes.

The preheat only pays off if the surface is in good shape, so it's worth learning [how to season a cast iron skillet properly](/how-to-season-cast-iron-skillet-properly/) first.

## The temperature you're actually aiming for

Most preheating advice never tells you what "hot" means, which is annoying, because there's a real number attached.

Browning is the Maillard reaction, and it needs the food's surface to get hot, not just warm. A 2022 study in *Foods* comparing reverse-seared and conventionally seared beef steaks puts it plainly: ["The temperature of the meat's surface should exceed 150 °C to attain the desired brown crust."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9317986/) That's about 302°F on the outside of your chicken thigh, not in the middle of it.

Here's the catch. Cold food dumps heat into the pan the second it lands, so the pan has to start hotter than the target. Those same researchers seared their control steaks on a pan preheated to 200 °C, roughly 392°F. Aim for the pan and the food takes care of itself.

## When to add the oil

For most cooking in stainless steel or cast iron, you heat the pan dry.

If you put the oil in a cold pan and let it sit through a five minute preheat, it may start smoking before the metal is evenly hot. A dry skillet surface clears 365°F on the way up and keeps climbing while the burner is on. Expeller-pressed sunflower passes its measured smoke point at 228°F and expeller-pressed peanut at 287°F, both long before the pan is ready, with refined coconut at 385°F not far behind. The full picture is on our [cooking oil smoke point chart](/cooking-oils-smoke-points-best-uses/). Burnt oil tastes bitter and drags down whatever you cook in it.

So let the dry pan get hot instead. How do you know it's ready? Use the water drop test. Flick a tiny drop of water off your fingers into the pan. If the water sizzles and immediately disappears, the pan isn't hot enough. If it beads up into a little ball and skitters wildly around the surface like a marble, the pan is ready.

That's the Leidenfrost effect, and it comes with a number. Researchers at Northumbria, Durham and Nottingham Trent, writing in *Materials Letters* in 2016, timed water droplets on a heated plate and found [the onset of the levitating regime at 185°C plus or minus 5](https://irep.ntu.ac.uk/id/eprint/27746/1/PubSub5201_Newton.pdf), which is about 365°F. Their surface was polished stainless steel, and roughness moves that threshold, so read it as a ballpark for a seasoned cast iron pan rather than a promise. It's still useful: a pan that passes the test sits well above the 302°F your food's surface needs, with room to spare for the drop when cold food lands.

Once the pan passes the water test, add your oil. Give it a few seconds. You'll see it shimmer and move fluidly across the bottom. Now add your food. You should hear a loud, aggressive sizzle. If it doesn't sizzle loudly, the pan wasn't ready. That sizzle is the whole difference between a juicy chop and a gray one, which is why the [best way to cook pork chops](/best-way-to-cook-pork-chops/) starts with this exact preheat.

## The nonstick exception

Everything above applies to bare metal. Nonstick is a different animal, and this is the one part of preheating that's a safety question rather than a texture question.

The National Capital Poison Center's prevention list for so-called Teflon flu is short and blunt: inspect your pans for chips and scratches, keep the kitchen well ventilated, and ["Do not preheat nonstick cookware."](https://www.poison.org/articles/teflon-flu) Not on high, not on medium, not empty at all. The same page says coated cookware "is intended for low to medium heat" and that the coating "starts to break down when exposed to temperatures above about 500 °F." An empty pan is exactly where a burner blows past that, because nothing is sitting there absorbing the energy.

So with nonstick, the fat and the food go in cold, the burner comes on at medium or lower, and the vent hood runs. Give it about a minute. You weren't getting a restaurant style sear out of that pan anyway. Nonstick is for eggs and pancakes, and those don't want 400°F.

## Stop touching the food

Once you've preheated the pan, added the oil, and placed your food down, you have to execute the hardest step of all. You have to walk away.

Don't poke it. Don't nudge it. Don't peek underneath after thirty seconds. Flip a piece of chicken before the crust has formed and it'll stick and tear. When the sear is done and the crust is golden brown, the food tends to release with very little resistance. That patience scales up to the expensive cuts too. The [best way to cook prime rib](/best-way-to-cook-prime-rib/) ends with a hard sear instead of starting with one, and a properly preheated pan is half that battle.

Preheating a skillet requires zero special equipment and costs zero dollars. It just requires you to stand there for three minutes before you start cooking. It's the cheapest, easiest way to make your weeknight dinners look and taste like you actually know what you're doing.
