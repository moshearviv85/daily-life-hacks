I used to end the argument at the lentil bag. 77.7 grams of protein per dollar, case closed, go soak something. Then I multiplied by the quality score and bone-in drumsticks walked past it by about a gram. That's annoying, because the bag is still the better raw deal, and the drumsticks only win after you dock the lentils.

This page is that dock, on ten foods. Raw grams come from plant-protein-per-dollar-ranked-2026.csv and animal-protein-per-dollar-ranked-2026.csv. The scores and the adjusted grams come from protein-quality-per-dollar-2026.csv. All three are named here in plain text. The wider 25-food ranking is [protein per dollar adjusted for quality](/protein-per-dollar-adjusted-for-quality/). October prices by census region are a separate question on [regional grocery protein prices](/us-regional-grocery-protein-prices-october-2026/).

## Which gives cheaper usable protein per dollar: plant staples or animal foods?

Bone-in chicken drumsticks, at [50.3](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/172373/nutrients) usable grams per dollar. Dry brown lentils lead the raw list at 77.7 grams, and a DIAAS of 0.63 drops them to 49.0. Drumsticks keep 50.3 because the score is capped at 1.00 under the [FAO 2013](https://openknowledge.fao.org/handle/20.500.14283/i3124e) method. Chickpeas finish at 47.1, split peas at 42.9, and large eggs at 34.4.

Usable here means the file's adjusted column, not a promise about your Tuesday. DIAAS is Digestible Indispensable Amino Acid Score. It grades whether a food's essential amino acids cover the pattern, and how digestible that protein is. A score of 1.00 is the ceiling this math allows. Anything published above that gets clipped before the multiplication, so a great score can't invent grams the price didn't buy.

## Ten foods, raw grams beside the adjusted grams

The adjusted column is raw grams times the capped score, rounded to one decimal the way the quality file already stores it. 77.7 times 0.63 lands at 49.0. 56.7 times 0.83 lands at 47.1. 73.9 times 0.58 lands at 42.9. 13.6 times 0.90 lands at 12.2. Foods at or above 1.00 keep the raw number.

The drumstick row already subtracts bone. The animal ranking notes a 33 percent refuse for bone and cartilage on [FDC 172373](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/172373/nutrients), and the 50.3 grams are what's left. Don't knock another third off because the package looks bony. The quality file also tags that 1.08 as a cooked-chicken proxy (Burd et al. 2019), not a lab score run on this exact bag of drumsticks. Mozzarella's 1.14 is tagged as a milk-protein proxy. The cap clips both to 1.00, so the proxy only matters if the real score for that food would have landed under 1.00. This page doesn't have that measurement. It has the stored one.

## Why the lentil lead disappears

On raw grams, dry brown lentils beat every meat in the two ranking files that this cut uses. 77.7 versus 50.3 for drumsticks. Split peas at 73.9 and chickpeas at 56.7 beat the drumsticks too. The plant ranking is the longer list of that aisle. The animal ranking, before any quality cut, is [animal protein per dollar](/animal-protein-per-dollar-ranked/), and drumsticks lead that one.

The quality file then multiplies. Lentils are limited by the sulfur amino acids, and the stored score is 0.63, so a third of the raw grams come off. Drumsticks lose nothing. 49.0 is a step behind 50.3, not a different sport. If your dinner is the bag, you're about a gram per dollar short of the drumsticks on this estimate, and you're ahead of eggs by a lot.

The stored scores aren't one paper. Cooked green lentils scored 0.58, cooked chickpeas 0.67, and split red lentils 0.50 in [Nosworthy et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28748078/). Cooked navy, black, and pinto beans scored 0.57, 0.63, and 0.70 in [Nosworthy et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6024599/). The 0.63 lentil figure and the 0.83 chickpea figure in this table don't match those papers. The quality file's own source text names Nosworthy for the lentil and Phillips 2017 for the chickpea. Read the numbers as the audited table, then read the papers as the reason not to treat 0.63 as a law of nature. [FAO Food and Nutrition Paper 92 (2013)](https://openknowledge.fao.org/handle/20.500.14283/i3124e) is the method: score the indispensable amino acids, and don't credit a food with more than a full match.

## Eggs, milk, and the beef that stays expensive

Eggs, mozzarella, whole milk, chicken breast, and 80/20 ground beef all publish a score above 1.00. The cap sets each one to 1.00, so the adjusted column copies the raw column. Eggs stay 34.4. Milk stays 28.5. Breast stays 24.6. Beef stays 11.3. Nobody in that group got cheaper. The lentils got discounted, and the eggs looked better by standing still.

Extra-firm tofu is the plant row that doesn't get to tell that story. It's 13.6 raw grams per dollar, a score of 0.90, and 12.2 adjusted ([FDC 174290](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/174290/nutrients)). Soy is the complete plant protein in the file's notes. Complete doesn't mean cheap. Ground beef at 11.3 is the only food in this ten that tofu still beats after the cut, and that's a low bar to clear. I wouldn't buy tofu to win a spreadsheet.

A plate can cover the holes the single foods have. Beans run short on the sulfur amino acids. Grains run short on lysine. [Beans and rice](/beans-and-rice-complete-protein-meal/) is the ordinary way kitchens have filled both holes, and it costs less than arguing about which single food hit 1.00. That pairing isn't in the ten-row table. The table is one food at a time, which is the honest way to price a bag, and a slightly silly way to price dinner.

If you cook the lentils, the bag still buys the most raw protein per dollar in this cut, and it finishes one step behind drumsticks after the cap. If you want the usable-gram lead the file actually stores, it's the drumsticks, bone already removed from the math, score already clipped at 1.00. Both sentences can be true. They just aren't the same sentence.
