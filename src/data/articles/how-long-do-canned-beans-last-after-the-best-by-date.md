USDA's shelf-stable food chart puts low-acid canned goods, and that includes every bean in your pantry, at [2 to 5 years on the shelf](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/shelf-stable-food). The printed date is not the end of that window. USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service says flatly that dates are about quality, not safety, and that a food which passes its date during home storage should still be safe if handled properly until spoilage is evident.

Which means the can in the back of the cabinet is asking you a different question than you thought. Not "what year is it," but "is this can intact."

| Canned food type | Unopened on the shelf | After opening |
|---|---|---|
| Low-acid: beans, corn, carrots, peas, meat, soups | 2 to 5 years | 3 to 4 days refrigerated |
| High-acid: tomatoes, fruit, juices, pickles | 12 to 18 months | 5 to 7 days refrigerated |
| Home canned foods | 12 months | 3 to 4 days refrigerated |

*Source: [USDA FSIS Shelf-Stable Food Safety](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/shelf-stable-food), last updated October 2024.*

## What does the best by date on canned beans actually mean?

Less than the packaging implies. FSIS's [food product dating guidance](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/food-product-dating) says a "Best if Used By/Before" date "indicates when a product will be of best flavor or quality. It is not a purchase or safety date." A "Sell-By" date is inventory management for the store. A "Use-By" date is the last date for peak quality, and is only a safety date on infant formula.

FSIS goes further and says it recommends manufacturers use "Best if Used By" specifically to reduce food waste, noting USDA estimates 30 percent of the food supply is lost or wasted at the retail and consumer levels, partly because people throw away good food out of confusion about dates.

The stamped code on the can lid is a different animal again. FSIS says can codes identify the date and time of production for recall tracking and "are not meant for the consumer to interpret as a 'Best if Used By' date."

## When should you throw a can of beans away?

When the container is the problem, not the calendar. FSIS gives a specific list of things that mean stop:

* **Bulging or swollen cans.** Discard, do not open.
* **Deep dents.** FSIS defines a deep dent as one you can lay your finger into, and says to discard any can with a deep dent on a seam. A small dent on an otherwise sound can is fine.
* **Heavy rust.** Surface rust you can rub off with a paper towel is not serious. Heavily rusted cans can have pinholes. If there's rust inside the can, don't eat the food.
* **Leaking, cracked jars, loose lids.**
* **A loud hiss or a forceful spurt on opening.** A soft hiss from vacuum pressure is normal.
* **Foul odor, or milky liquid around vegetables that should be clear.**

FSIS's instruction on all of these is the same and it's worth repeating: do not taste the food. Double bag suspect cans and put them in the regular trash where animals and children can't reach them.

## Does the storage spot matter more than the date?

Considerably. FSIS says temperatures over 100F are harmful to canned goods and that the risk of spoilage jumps sharply as storage temperatures rise, recommending below 85F, and specifically warning against storing cans above or beside the stove, under the sink, or in a damp garage or basement. [Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/food-safety/emergency-food-supply) tightens that to 50 to 70 degrees.

[Utah State University Extension](https://extension.usu.edu/preserve-the-harvest/research/storing-canned-goods) adds a detail I had never thought about: store metal cans off the floor, especially bare concrete, because moisture wicks up and starts rust.

Two years of a garage summer will age a can faster than five years in a hall closet. The date on the lid does not know where you kept it.

## Is stocking up on cans actually the cheap move?

For convenience, cans are excellent. For value, dry beans win by a distance that surprises people every time.

In our July 2026 price sample, canned black beans delivered 30.1 grams of protein per dollar, canned kidney beans 23.4, and canned chickpeas 22.0. Dry pinto beans delivered 97.9. That's about three times more protein per dollar for the bag, which is the whole argument in [canned vs dry beans on cost](/canned-vs-dry-beans-cost/), and the same reason [dried beans anchor a cheap pantry](/how-much-food-storage-do-you-need-per-person/).
