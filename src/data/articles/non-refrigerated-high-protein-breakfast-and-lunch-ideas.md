The cooler is in the other truck. Or there isn't a cooler, and the fridge at work is a rumor somebody started in 2019. You still need breakfast that might hold you past 10, and a lunch that doesn't ask a microwave for permission.

These eight boxes are the non-refrigerated version. Each one clears 20 g of protein and 8 g of fiber. The peanut-butter banana sandwich is $0.87. The sardine can is $1.56. Sealed jars, sealed cans, bread, fruit, and a bag of popped corn can ride at room temperature. An opened can of fish can't. [USDA FSIS](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/danger-zone-40f-140f) gives perishable food 2 hours above 40°F, or 1 hour above 90°F.

## What belongs in a non-refrigerated high-protein lunch?

Food that doesn't need a cold pack while the package is closed, and a can you open when you eat. That's the whole rule. Peanut butter leads the protein-per-dollar shelf at 50.7 g per dollar ([FDC 174266](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/174266/nutrients)). The fish is the expensive protein that still fits the band. Popcorn is here for fiber, not for glory.

This isn't the raw grocery inventory. That cart, including the foods that still need a fridge, is the [no-cook emergency protein and fiber list](/no-cook-emergency-protein-and-fiber-grocery-list/). It also isn't a reheated thermos lunch. Those packed days are [healthy blue collar lunch ideas](/healthy-blue-collar-lunch-ideas-men/). If your morning is more about lasting energy than about a cab without a cooler, the breakfast plate with the USDA numbers is [breakfast foods for sustained energy](/best-breakfast-foods-for-sustained-energy/).

## Breakfast that can sit on the counter

Eat the jar boxes early or at noon. I'm not inventing a clock for peanut butter. The jar is the salted one, 22.21 g of protein and 5.0 g of fiber per 100 g. USDA's slice of this bread is 32 g. Every box uses 52 g, the day-menu two slices. fiber-day-cost-2026.csv still prices those 52 g at $0.18. The current files price a 20 oz loaf at $3.52, the May 2026 BLS average, which is $0.32 for the same 52 g ([FDC 172688](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/172688/nutrients)). I used $0.32.

The medium banana is 118 g edible, $0.26, with the peel kept in the price through the 0.64 edible fraction ([FDC 173944](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173944/nutrients)). Seventy grams of sunflower kernels is half of the 140 g cup ([FDC 170562](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/170562/nutrients)). That jar and that bag are the portable end of the [high-protein snack ranking](/cheapest-high-protein-snacks-and-portable-foods/). Here they just sit on bread.

Prunes are pocket fiber, 7.1 g per 100 g, and only 2.18 g of protein ([FDC 168162](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168162/nutrients)). The apple is the watery version: 180 g edible gala is 200 g before the core comes off, because the edible fraction is 0.9, and that flesh costs $0.56 ([FDC 168204](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168204/nutrients)).

## The cans are lunch, and they have a clock

Chunk light tuna in water, drained, is the whole 113 g can. That's 22.0 g of protein, no fiber, and $0.98 ([FDC 173709](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173709/nutrients)). Sardines in oil, drained solids with bone, are the whole 92 g can. That's 22.7 g of protein, no fiber, and $1.12 ([FDC 175139](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/175139/nutrients)). The fish is why those two boxes jump to $1.50 and $1.56. The popcorn and the bread are what get the fiber over 8 g.

Open the can when you eat. A sealed can doesn't care about the cab. An opened can is perishable. FSIS wants it cold, at or below 40°F, within 2 hours, and that window shrinks to 1 hour above 90°F. I won't call a warm tin in the cup holder a meal-prep strategy. Eat it. Don't save a heroic tablespoon for later unless you've got a fridge.

## Popcorn is the cheap fiber, with a footnote

The kernel row returns 51.3 g of fiber per dollar, and it has no USDA id. The eaten weight is air-popped popcorn, [FDC 167959](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/167959/nutrients), 14.5 g of fiber and 12.94 g of protein per 100 g. The fiber file's 12.9 g kernel figure comes from that popped record. I priced popped grams as the kernel price times 14.5 divided by 12.9, about $0.28 per 100 g. Forty grams is 5.8 g of fiber and $0.11.

Pop it at home. Don't pack raw kernels and call it lunch. Peanuts do the same fiber job without the footnote, 8.4 g per 100 g, and 70 g of them is 17.0 g of protein ([FDC 174262](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/174262/nutrients)). Popcorn still got three boxes. Eleven cents for almost 6 g of fiber is a rude little bargain.

## Where these numbers come from

Protein rates for the jar, the nuts, the bread, and the cans are protein-per-dollar-2026.csv, and the same rates show up in no-cook-protein-per-dollar-2026.csv or shelf-stable-pantry-per-dollar-2026.csv where those files name the food. Fiber and package prices are fiber-per-dollar-2026.csv. high-fiber-snacks-per-dollar-2026.csv repeats the fiber rate for peanut butter, peanuts, prunes, and apples. Fruit, fish, and popped-popcorn protein are the USDA records linked above. The 52 g bread weight is the day menu. Its $0.32 is the BLS loaf, not the older $0.18. Rounded half up. The filenames stay in the sentence. This page doesn't link the files.
